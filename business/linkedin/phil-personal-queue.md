### 15 Nov — plot checks
You've found the plot and the price works. Should you pay the deposit?

Not yet. On a Dubai villa plot, the price is the easiest thing to check. What decides what you can actually build sits in other documents:

• The affection plan: area, boundaries and permitted use
• The master developer's design guidelines: setbacks, height, coverage and style
• Build-by deadlines, and what happens if you miss them
• Ground conditions, dewatering and utility connections

Each one can change the villa you can build, or what it costs to build it.

Then run a realistic build budget alongside the plot price before you commit, not after.

### 17 Nov — renovate or rebuild
Renovate your Dubai villa, or knock it down and rebuild? Most owners decide on cost per square foot. That's rarely what settles it.

Rebuilding tends to win when:

• The structure, services or waterproofing are failing
• The plot allows meaningfully more floor area
• The layout simply can't be made to work

One thing owners often miss: on a rebuild, the family can usually stay in the old villa through design and approvals, so the months out of the house are fewer than you'd think.

Decide on evidence. A condition survey and some opening-up first, then a cost plan for each option, before any design is fixed.

### 18 Nov — building from the UK
You live in the UK. Your villa is being built in Dubai. Which decisions can you safely make from home?

Most of them. A handful can't: the plot, the design freeze, the finishes and the handover.

For everything else, insist on three things, every week or month:

• A dated photo report
• An updated cost to complete
• A decision log showing what you approved, and when

And never release a payment you can't tie to an agreed milestone, however urgent the request sounds.

Overseas projects rarely fail through one big event. They fail through small, unrecorded changes nobody challenged at the time.

### 20 Nov — defects liability period
You've got the keys. Is the contractor finished with you? Not yet, and you shouldn't be finished with them.

The defects liability period, often twelve months on building works, is your window to find and report defects while the retention is still held. Use it:

• Test every wet area in daily use in the first month
• Run every AC unit for a full day in July and August
• Check roofs, terraces and drains after the first heavy rain
• Re-inspect the whole villa in month 10 or 11, before the retention is released

Beyond that, UAE law makes the contractor and engineer jointly liable for ten years for defects that threaten the building's stability or safety.

### 21 Nov — kitchen and bathroom costs
AED 15,000 or AED 150,000? That's the spread of published prices for a complete villa kitchen in Dubai.

The sources aren't wrong. They're pricing different things. What really moves the number:

• Whether the layout changes, and the plumbing and electrics move with it
• Custom joinery or off-the-shelf cabinets
• The stone and the appliances
• Developer approvals and community access permits

A new kitchen in the same footprint and a full reconfiguration are different projects, even in the same villa. Fix the scope before you compare quotes. Then the numbers mean something.

### 22 Nov — choosing a contractor
Three villa bids on the table. Do you take the lowest? For 21 years I was the one pricing them, and the lowest bid is rarely the cheapest build.

Before you compare totals, compare the same things:

• Preliminaries, and what share of the total they are
• Provisional sums, and whether they're realistic
• Exclusions, priced back in
• Programme, payment terms, bonds and retention

Then ask who will actually run your job, and go and see their last villas.

It works both ways. Good contractors price sharper for a client with complete drawings, a fair contract and someone on their side who understands construction.

### 23 Nov — owner's rep vs PM vs consultant
Who on your villa project works only for you? Ask that before you appoint anyone.

Registered consultant, project manager, owner's representative. They can sound like the same job. They aren't:

• Your registered consultant designs the villa, makes the authority submissions and has the statutory role on site
• A project manager serves whoever appoints them, and that isn't always you
• An owner's representative works for the owner only, with no stake in the design or the build

One question sorts them quickly: who else pays you on this project? For an owner's rep, the only acceptable answer is nobody.

### 24 Nov — which authority
Dubai Municipality, Trakhees or the Dubai Development Authority: which one approves your villa? If you're going by the community name, you could be wrong.

It's set by your plot, not the brochure. And it changes:

• Your approvals route
• Your programme
• Sometimes your design

One more thing that catches owners out: in almost every villa community, the master developer's approval comes before the authority permit. That step often sets the timeline more than the permit does.

My rule: confirm the authority with your registered consultant from the plot documents before design starts. Never assume it.

### 25 Nov — construction contracts (replaces "sourcing materials abroad", removed at Phil's request)
Your villa contract is signed. Will it protect you when the first change or delay arrives?

Most disputes I've seen didn't start with a bad contract. They started with a reasonable one that nobody administered. Before you sign, know:

• Lump sum or remeasure: it decides who carries the risk of quantities changing
• How variations are instructed: in writing, and priced before the work is done
• How delays are notified, and what records back an extension of time
• When the performance bond expires. One that expires before completion protects no one

Dubai Municipality publishes guidance contract forms for private villas. They're in Arabic, not mandatory, and can be amended by agreement.

General information, not legal advice: take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer before you sign.
