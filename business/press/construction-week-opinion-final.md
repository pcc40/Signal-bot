# Why villa budgets leak, and how owners can catch it early

*Incomplete allowances and unpriced changes can leave villa owners committed to costs they have yet to see. The warning signs are often in the paperwork.*

By Philip Chapman

Ask an owner why their villa went over budget and you will usually hear about one moment. The rock under the pool. The stone that doubled in price. The month the site went quiet. Those events matter. But in my experience, the gap often starts earlier, in a tender that looks more complete than it is.

I founded P Chapman Construction in Cornwall in 2005 and have spent 21 years pricing and building one-off homes. When a tender landed on my desk, I read the drawings for what was missing before I read them for what was there. Unresolved details had to be allowed for, excluded or clarified, and the owner needed to understand those qualifications as well as the price.

That experience shapes how I look at villa budgets in Dubai today. A signed contract can give an owner confidence without giving them the cost certainty they think they have bought.

Start with the allowances. Kitchens, joinery and lighting often appear in a tender as provisional sums because the specification is unfinished. The money is in the price, but the eventual cost is still open. A tender with realistic allowances can look expensive beside one that leaves the same items underfunded, and the cheaper-looking bid wins.

Before comparing totals, put those allowances on the same basis. Ask what each one covers. Supply only, or installation too? Are fittings and the contractor's mark-up included? What specification supports the figure? If the kitchen you intend to buy already costs more than the allowance, that difference belongs in the forecast now. Better to know it today than to find out at handover.

Check what sits outside the building contract as well. Landscaping, utility connections and professional fees still need funding, whoever procures them. A tender can be within budget while the cost of actually finishing the home is already beyond it.

Time needs the same scrutiny. Preliminaries cover the cost of running the site: management, security, welfare, temporary power. Turner & Townsend's 2025 UAE market intelligence reports Dubai preliminaries of 12 per cent on a 2,500 sq m project. That is a general benchmark, not a villa quotation, and not all of it repeats when a programme slips. Some costs are fixed. Others keep running for as long as the site is open.

So the useful question is which costs continue, and who is responsible for the delay under the contract. Put the owner's own decisions on the programme, with dates, alongside the construction activities. A tile selection made after its order deadline can hold up delivery and every trade that follows. Owners should be able to see that before the decision becomes late, not after.

Changes on site are the most familiar source of trouble, and they usually happen on a good day. The owner walks through, sees the rooms taking shape and asks to move a wall or upgrade the windows. The team agrees to get on with it. The cost conversation waits until the work is done and there is little room left to reconsider.

I have been on the contractor's side of plenty of those conversations. An owner may hear agreement to a change as agreement that it is minor. The contractor may hear an instruction to proceed at extra cost. Both are acting in good faith. Write down the scope, the price and the effect on completion before authorising the work. Where something urgent has to proceed before a price is settled, record the instruction and agree how it will be valued. Either way, keep unresolved changes visible in the forecast.

Payments need the same care. Agreed advances and payments for materials can be perfectly legitimate, so money paid will not always match work installed. Each application should still be checked against the contract and supported by evidence. The owner needs to know what has been paid for, where it is and what protection applies. An unexplained lead over progress leaves less money to finish with if the contractor gets into difficulty.

Each month, ask for the expected final cost against the approved budget, including committed spend, changes still waiting to be agreed and a realistic allowance for the risks that remain. A report showing only invoices paid tells you where the money has gone. It does not tell you whether you can afford to finish.

Keep the contingency separate, and review it as risks become clearer. Its size should reflect how complete the design and surveys are. Spending it on a kitchen that was under-allowed from the start leaves less for work that is genuinely unforeseen.

Most contractors want a properly defined job and a settled final account as much as their clients do. Owners have a part in making that possible: timely decisions, clear instructions and someone with explicit responsibility for checking costs. A quantity surveyor, project manager or lead consultant may carry that responsibility, depending on the appointment. What matters is that somebody has it.

Before signing, ask what is still unpriced. During the build, ask what the house is now expected to cost at completion. Those two answers will tell you more than any reassurance that the job is still on budget.
