# Villa budgets don't blow up. They leak.

**A builder who spent 21 years pricing private houses explains where an owner's money goes on a Dubai villa, and how to see it earlier.**

By Philip Chapman, founding partner, Eastbridge Strategic Partners

---

When a private villa in Dubai finishes well over budget, the owner usually remembers one moment: the structural surprise, the marble that doubled in price, the month the site went quiet. In my experience that moment is rarely where the money went. Budgets on one-off houses don't blow up. They leak, a line at a time, and most of the leaks are visible months before anyone feels them.

I know this because for 21 years I was the one pricing them. I ran a premium residential building company in the UK, building one-off contemporary homes for private clients. We priced tenders, managed variations and, when we had to, argued about time and money in adjudication. I was not the villain of the story, and nor are most contractors. But I learned exactly which lines in a tender carry the risk, and which ones clients almost never read.

## The leak starts before the contract

The first leak is an incomplete design. A contractor can only price what it is shown. Where the drawings stop, the tender fills the gap with provisional sums and allowances: an amount for the pool, an amount for the joinery, an amount for the lighting control system. They look like costs. They are really placeholders, and they are often set at a level that wins the job rather than one that reflects what the owner has in mind.

When the real design arrives, the provisional sum is replaced with a real price, and the difference is the owner's. On a villa with a dozen allowances, that difference can be the whole contingency before a single wall goes up. Ask how each allowance was calculated, and what the specification behind it is. If nobody can tell you, it is a guess, and you are carrying it.

## Time is money, literally

The second leak is the programme. Preliminaries, the contractor's site running costs (management, welfare, security, temporary power, equipment), are priced per week. Turner & Townsend's 2025 UAE market intelligence puts preliminaries on a 2,500 m² project in Dubai at around 12% of construction cost. That is a large line, and it grows with every week the job runs over.

Delay on a villa is rarely dramatic. It is a late decision on tiles, a drawing revision waiting for approval, a long-lead item ordered a month too late, a Ramadan or a summer midday break nobody planned around. Each costs a week. Some of those weeks are the contractor's risk. Many are the owner's, because they come from the owner's side of the table: decisions, design changes, consultant information. If the contract allows time and money for those delays, the preliminaries follow.

## Variations agreed with a handshake

The third leak happens on site. An owner visits, sees the room taking shape, and asks for a change: move a wall, add a socket run, upgrade a window. The site manager says yes. Nobody writes down the price or the effect on the programme. Six months later the change arrives on the final account, priced by the contractor, with time attached.

None of this is dishonest. It is how building works when changes aren't controlled. The cure is dull and effective: no change proceeds without a written price and a stated effect on time, approved by the owner before the work starts.

## Paying ahead of the work

The fourth leak is cash flow. Payment applications are valued against progress. If they are not checked against what is actually on site, an owner can find that payments have run ahead of the build. That is a serious problem if a contractor gets into difficulty, and a common thread in the stalled villa projects owners talk about. Each month, someone on the owner's side should walk the site with the application in hand and ask: is this work done, and is it done properly?

## The fix is process, not suspicion

I don't think owners need to distrust their contractor. They need what any well-run commercial project already has.

**A frozen brief before tender.** Every allowance either replaced with a real specification or understood for what it is.

**A programme that shows the owner's own decisions**, with dates, so that the owner knows which delays will be theirs.

**A priced change log.** Every variation recorded, priced and approved in writing before it is built.

**A monthly cost report** against the original budget, showing committed cost, approved changes and what is left.

**A contingency that is actually held.** Industry guides commonly recommend 10 to 15% of project cost for a villa. It is there for the unknown, not to absorb a design that was never finished.

Dubai's villa market has excellent consultants and capable contractors. What private owners often lack is someone on their side of the table who reads the tender, the programme and the payment application the way the contractor does. Larger developers have that as a matter of course. A family building one home usually doesn't.

Budgets leak where nobody is looking. The good news is that, if you know where to look, almost every leak can be seen in time to stop it.
