# Why villa budgets leak, and how owners can catch it early

*Private owners in Dubai rarely lose money in one dramatic moment. A builder who spent 21 years pricing one-off homes explains where it goes instead.*

By Philip Chapman, founding partner, Eastbridge Strategic Partners

Ask an owner whose villa came in well over budget what went wrong, and you will usually hear about one moment. The rock under the pool. The stone that doubled in price. The month the site went quiet. Those moments happen, but in my experience they rarely account for the gap. Most of the overspend arrives quietly, a line at a time, and much of it could have been seen months earlier by anyone who knew which pages to read.

I spent 21 years running a building company in the UK, pricing and building one-off houses for private clients. When a tender landed on my desk, I read the drawings for what was missing before I read them for what was there. Every builder does, and nobody in the trade would call it sharp practice. It is how you stay in business. But it means the contractor's team knows where the money is likely to move long before the owner does.

The first place is the design itself. A contractor can only price what it is shown, and where the drawings stop, the tender fills the gap with provisional sums: an allowance for the kitchen, one for the joinery, another for the lighting control. On paper they look like costs. In practice they are placeholders, often set at whatever level helps the bid look competitive. When the owner finally chooses the kitchen they actually want, the allowance is swapped for a real price and the difference lands with them. On a luxury villa with a dozen of these, that difference can swallow the contingency before the frame is up. Ask who set each allowance and what specification sits behind it. If nobody can tell you, it's a guess, and you are the one carrying it.

The second is time. A contractor's site running costs, its preliminaries, are mostly priced by the week: site management, security, welfare, temporary power, plant. Turner & Townsend's 2025 UAE market intelligence puts preliminaries at about 12% of construction cost on a 2,500 m² project in Dubai. That is a large number to have ticking over. Villa programmes seldom collapse; they drift. A late decision on tiles. A drawing revision waiting for comment. A long-lead item ordered a few weeks too late. A Ramadan nobody planned around. Some of that drift is the contractor's problem. A surprising amount sits on the owner's side, and where the contract gives time and money for it, the weekly costs follow.

The third leak opens on site, usually on a good day. The owner walks through, sees the rooms taking shape and asks for a change: shift a wall, add a run of sockets, upgrade the windows. The site manager says yes, because that is what site managers do. Nobody writes down a price, or what it does to the programme. Months later it reappears on the final account, priced by the people who did the work, with time attached. I have been on the other side of plenty of those conversations. They are rarely anyone's fault, and they are almost always avoidable.

The fourth is cash. Monthly payment applications are supposed to track the work. If nobody on the owner's side checks them against what is physically on site, payments can drift ahead of progress. That does little harm while the job is going well. If the contractor runs into difficulty, it can be the difference between a delay and a stalled project with the owner's money already spent.

Owners don't need to treat their contractor as an opponent. Most contractors I have worked with want a clean job and a settled final account as much as their client does. What owners need is the discipline any commercial developer would treat as standard. Finish the design before tender, or at least set the allowances yourself at realistic levels so every bidder prices the same thing. Put the owner's own decisions on the programme, with dates, so everyone can see which delays would be yours. Agree the price and time effect of every change in writing before the work starts. Ask for a short cost report each month showing what is committed, what has changed and what is left. And hold a real contingency: published Dubai cost guides commonly recommend 10 to 15% of project cost. It is there for the unknown. It shouldn't be paying for a design that was never finished.

Dubai has excellent consultants and capable contractors, and the villas going up here are as ambitious as any house I built in the UK. The gap I see is on the owner's side of the table. A large developer has someone reading every tender, programme and valuation the way the contractor does. A family building one house usually has nobody doing that job, and that is where the money goes.

*Philip Chapman is founding partner of Eastbridge Strategic Partners, an independent client-side project manager for villas in Dubai and Abu Dhabi. For 21 years he ran P Chapman Construction, a UK builder whose Owers House won a RIBA South West Award in 2016 and featured on Channel 4's Grand Designs.*
