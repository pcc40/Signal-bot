## 2. The article (paste from the title down; about 1,250 words of body text)

Title: **Owner's representative**

---

An owner's representative (often shortened to owner's rep, and sometimes called an owner's project manager or client-side project manager) is a person or firm appointed by the owner of a building project to manage the project from the owner's side and to act only in the owner's interest. The owner's representative does not design or build. Their role is to coordinate the consultants and contractor, control cost and programme, and help the owner make informed decisions at each stage.

The term is most widely used in North America and in parts of the Middle East and Asia. In the UK, similar functions are usually described as client's representative, project manager, employer's agent or independent client adviser, and the precise scope depends on the appointment rather than the title.

The role is defined by contract, not by statute. Its content therefore varies from one appointment to the next, and the written scope of services, the authority delegated by the owner and the fee basis matter more than the job title.

# How the role differs from related roles

The owner's representative overlaps with several established roles. The main differences are:

- Project manager. Project management describes an activity rather than an allegiance. A project manager may be employed by the client, the consultant or the contractor. An owner's representative is a project manager appointed by, and answerable only to, the owner.
- Employer's agent. Under some UK forms of contract, particularly design and build, the employer's agent is a named contractual role that administers the contract for the employer. An owner's representative may also act as employer's agent or contract administrator if the contract names them, but the two are not the same: one is a role within a construction contract, the other a role defined by the owner's own appointment.
- Client's representative. In UK usage this is the closest equivalent. The client's representative manages the project for the client, may come from inside the client organisation or be an external consultant, and should have clearly defined delegated authority.
- Clerk of works or site inspector. A clerk of works inspects the works and reports, usually to the contract administrator. An owner's representative will normally visit site and report, but the role extends to cost, programme, procurement and decisions.
- Quantity surveyor. A quantity surveyor (cost consultant) provides specialist cost advice: cost plans, tender documents, valuations and final accounts. An owner's representative uses cost information to manage the project and may appoint or work with a quantity surveyor rather than replace one.
- Design-and-build contractor. A design-and-build contractor designs, prices and builds under a single contract. An owner's representative is sometimes appointed alongside a design-and-build contract to give the owner an independent view of the design, the price and any changes.

The owner's representative does not take on the design responsibilities of the architect or engineers, the statutory responsibilities of designers or contractors, or the contractor's responsibility for the works.

# Typical duties

Duties depend on the appointment but commonly cover:

- Briefing: helping the owner set the brief, budget, programme and decision schedule before significant design fees are committed.
- Procurement: advising on the procurement route, helping the owner select and appoint consultants and contractors, and comparing tenders on a like-for-like basis.
- Contract: reviewing appointments and the construction contract with the owner and the owner's lawyers, so that scope, payment, change and handover terms are clear.
- Construction: tracking cost and programme, reviewing payment applications and variations before the owner pays, visiting site, chairing or attending progress meetings, keeping a record of the owner's decisions and reporting regularly in plain terms.
- Handover: coordinating snagging, testing and commissioning records, as-built information, warranties and operating manuals.
- Defects period: following up defects reported during the defects liability period and helping the owner prepare for release of retention and agreement of the final account.

# Independence and conflicts of interest

The value of the role rests on independence. A conflict of interest can arise where the person checking the work has a stake in it, for example a contractor-employed project manager reviewing the contractor's own claims, a designer assessing variations caused by gaps in their own design, or an adviser who receives commission or referral fees from consultants, contractors or suppliers.

Appointments therefore commonly state who the owner's representative acts for, confirm that they receive no payment from any other party on the project, and set out what they may instruct without the owner's approval. Many owners keep the authority to approve cost themselves.

# Fees

Fees are agreed between the owner and the owner's representative. Common bases include:

- A percentage of construction cost. This is simple to apply, but the fee rises with the cost of the building, which does not reward savings.
- A monthly or time-based fee against a defined scope, which links the fee to the work done rather than to construction value.
- A fixed fee for a defined task, such as a review of a contract, a tender comparison or a single payment application.

There are no mandatory fee scales. Fees vary with the size and complexity of the project, the frequency of site attendance and the reporting required, so proposals are best compared against the scope of services rather than on the headline figure.

# Residential and private-client use

Owner's representatives are most associated with large commercial and institutional projects, but they are also appointed on private houses, particularly bespoke new builds, major renovations and projects where the owner has not built before.

They are often used by owners building abroad or living away from the site, who cannot attend progress meetings, check work on site or review payment claims themselves. In these cases the owner's representative also manages communication across time zones and languages and keeps a written record of decisions.

On a straightforward project, an experienced owner who lives near the site and has a well-appointed consultant may not need one. The decision usually turns on whether independent control of cost, programme and change is likely to be worth more than the fee.

# In the UAE

In the United Arab Emirates, a private villa normally involves a registered engineering consultant, who designs the building, prepares the applications to the authorities and carries the statutory site role during construction, and a licensed main contractor. An owner's representative, where appointed, works alongside both and does not replace the consultant's statutory role. In Dubai, only registered consultancies may practise engineering consultancy, under Dubai Law No. 14 of 2025.

Which authority issues building permits depends on the location of the plot. In Dubai this may be Dubai Municipality, Trakhees (in certain special development zones) or the Dubai Development Authority, and many communities also require a no-objection certificate (NOC) from the master developer before an application is made. In Abu Dhabi, building permit services are accessed through the TAMM government services platform. Dubai Municipality publishes standard contract forms for consultancy and construction of private villas.

Ten-year (decennial) liability of the contractor and the engineer for collapse and for defects that threaten a building's stability or safety is set out in the UAE civil code. Federal Decree-Law No. 25 of 2025 on civil transactions, which replaced Federal Law No. 5 of 1985 from 1 June 2026, keeps this regime, moving it from Articles 880 to 883 to Articles 821 to 824. The liability cannot be excluded or limited by contract. Owners are advised to take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer on how it applies to their own contracts.
