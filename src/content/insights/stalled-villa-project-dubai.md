When your villa build stalls in Dubai, spend the first ten days establishing facts before taking positions: secure the site, preserve every record, pause any payment not yet properly valued until you know what has been built and what is genuinely owed, and read the contract with a UAE-qualified lawyer before you withhold sums the contract says are due or terminate anything. Only then choose between getting the current contractor back on track, renegotiating, or a managed change of contractor, which Dubai Municipality allows under a published procedure.

A stalled site is stressful because everyone has a different story. The contractor says it has not been paid. The consultant says the contractor is late. The owner has often paid well over half the contract sum for a building that looks less than half finished. We spent 21 years as the builder in UK premium residential construction. From the builder's side, we know how a stalled job looks from the contractor's chair, and what an incoming builder needs before it will price the rest. This is what we would want any owner to do, in order.

## Why do villa builds stall?

A stall is a symptom. Before deciding what to do, work out which of a small number of causes you are looking at, because each calls for a different response.

| What you see on site | What it usually means | The first question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Labour numbers falling, subcontractors absent | The contractor's cash flow is under strain, on your job or across its business | Have all valued payments been made, and is the contractor paying its own supply chain? |
| Work stopped in one area, waiting | Missing or late design information, or an unmade owner decision | Is there an outstanding request for information or selection, and whose is it? |
| A formal letter about suspension or delay | A dispute about payment, variations or time | What does the contract say about notices, suspension and extensions? |
| Work continuing but very slowly | Contractor over-committed elsewhere, or deliberately slowing to force a negotiation | What does the programme say, and when was it last updated? |
| Consultant unresponsive or not attending | A dispute between owner and consultant, or a consultant stretched too thin | Is the consultant's appointment and fee position in order? |
| Nobody on site at all | Abandonment, insolvency or a serious dispute | Has the contractor responded in writing, and is it still trading? |

On the builder's side, a slowing site was almost always a cash signal before it was a programme signal. Labour goes first, then the subcontractors stop returning calls, then the letters start. An owner who notices the first stage and asks the right questions early has far more options than one who waits for the letter.

## What should I do in the first ten days?

The checklist below is the order we recommend. It is deliberately conservative: nothing in it closes off an option, and everything in it improves your position whichever route you choose.

| Days | Action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Visit, photograph and video the whole site, dated | Fixes the state of the works before anything is moved, removed or disputed |
| 1–2 | Check site security, hoarding, safety and weather protection | An open, unprotected site deteriorates and creates liability |
| 1–3 | Write to the contractor asking for the reason and a return date | Puts the position on record and invites a reply you can rely on |
| 2–3 | Gather the contract, drawings, programme, valuations, payments and correspondence | You cannot assess anything you cannot find |
| 2–3 | Pause further payments until the position is checked | Money paid ahead of work is leverage lost |
| 3–5 | Read the termination, suspension, notice and dispute clauses with a lawyer | A wrongful termination can turn a strong position into a weak one |
| 3–5 | Confirm the permit, insurance and bond position | Expiring cover or guarantees cannot be recovered later |
| 5–7 | Ask your consultant for a technical assessment of progress and quality | It is the basis of any restart, renegotiation or changeover |
| 5–8 | Reconcile what has been paid against what has been built | Tells you whether you are ahead of or behind the work |
| 7–10 | Estimate the cost and time to complete, and set out the options | Turns a crisis into a decision |

### Days 1–2: secure the site and the record

Go to site, or send someone you trust. Photograph and video every room, elevation and service run, with dates. Note what materials are on site and what plant has been removed. Check the hoarding, access control, temporary power and water, and anything open to the weather, such as unprotected roof membranes, exposed reinforcement or stored finishes. Under many contracts these risks sit with the contractor while it is in possession of the site, but a deteriorating building is your problem whoever pays for it.

### Days 2–3: pause the money, not the conversation

Keep talking to the contractor, politely and in writing. Ask why work has stopped and when it will resume. But pause any payment not yet properly valued until you know where you stand. Pay only what has been properly valued and approved under the contract for work actually done, and take legal advice before withholding anything the contract says is due, because an unjustified non-payment can hand the contractor a right to suspend.

On the contractor's side, the request we dreaded most from a client was not an angry phone call. It was a calm letter asking for an updated programme, a list of outstanding information and a reconciliation of the account. Those three documents show quickly who is really behind.

### Days 3–5: read the contract before acting on it

Find the clauses on suspension, termination, notices, extensions of time, liquidated damages, retention, bonds and disputes. Notice periods matter: many contracts require a notice of default and a period to remedy it before termination is possible. This is general information, not legal advice; your contract and UAE law decide what you can do, so take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer at this point, not after you have sent a termination letter. Our guide to [villa construction contracts in the UAE](/insights/villa-construction-contracts-uae/) explains the main mechanisms, and if the dispute is about variations or time, see how we help with [variations, delays and claims](/services/variations-delays-and-claims/).

Check three time-sensitive items at the same time: whether the building permit and its conditions remain in order (ask your consultant), whether the contractor's all-risks and liability insurance is still in force, and when any [performance bond](/glossary/#performance-bond) or advance payment guarantee expires. A bond that lapses during a dispute protects no one.

### Days 5–8: establish what has actually been built

Ask your registered consultant for a technical assessment of the stage of the works and whether they conform to the permit and the approved drawings. Alongside it, reconcile the money: total valued, total paid, retention held, variations agreed and variations claimed. Compare that with what is physically in place. If the payments have run ahead of the work, you need to know by how much.

### Days 7–10: cost to complete, and the options

With the record and the reconciliation in hand, estimate what it will cost and how long it will take to finish. Then set out the options side by side: bring the current contractor back with a recovery programme, renegotiate scope or terms, or replace the contractor. A changeover carries real costs in time, re-pricing and risk.

## Should I terminate the contract?

Not in the first ten days, and not without legal advice. Termination is sometimes right, but it is a legal step with consequences, and a contractor that has been terminated wrongly may have a claim against you.

For context, a Gulf News legal column published in 2024 describes the general position under Article 877 of the UAE Civil Transactions Law: where a contractor performs defectively or contrary to the agreement, the owner may require it to put matters right within a reasonable time and, if it fails, may ask the court to cancel the contract or to authorise completion by another contractor at the first contractor's expense. How that applies to your contract is a question for a UAE-qualified lawyer.

## How do I change contractor on a Dubai villa?

Dubai Municipality's Administrative Resolution No. 10 of 2019 sets out the procedures for replacing an engineering consultant or a contractor on applications made to the municipality. In summary, as the English translation on the Dubai Legislation Portal describes it (the Arabic text prevails):

- **With the outgoing contractor's agreement:** your engineering consultant submits the application, signed by the consultant and the outgoing contractor, with a declaration from the owner approving the change and, where a permit has been issued, the consultant's technical report on the stage of the works and their conformity with the permit and approved plans.
- **Without its agreement:** the consultant submits the application with the owner's declaration, the reasons for the change and the technical report. The municipality notifies the outgoing contractor, which has five working days to give its reasons and its own technical report. If it does not respond, the municipality proceeds. Where the dispute is financial or contractual, the municipality proceeds with the replacement; where it is technical, the municipality verifies the issues first.
- **In both cases:** the replacement contractor must be licensed, with a classification not lower than the building requires, and **may not start work until a new building permit is issued in its name**, on payment of the prescribed fees and security.

Dubai Municipality publishes a request form for changing a consultant or contractor, with and without agreement, and its online building permit system includes the change of consultant or contractor among its services. Your consultant makes the application, through the [Build in Dubai](https://buildindubai.gov.ae/) platform or the authority's own system. If your permit was issued by Trakhees or the Dubai Development Authority rather than Dubai Municipality, the route may differ, so ask your consultant to confirm the procedure for your plot. In communities with a master developer, check whether the developer's own approvals also need updating.

## What about a stalled villa in Abu Dhabi?

The principles are the same: secure the site, preserve the record, pause unvalued payments, take legal advice and establish what has been built. The procedure is not. In Abu Dhabi the Department of Municipalities and Transport regulates private villas and permits are handled by the municipalities through TAMM. Ask your consultant to confirm the current requirements for changing a contractor on your permit before you commit to a changeover.

## What does an incoming contractor need to price the rest fairly?

An incoming contractor prices the unknown first: work it cannot see, quality it cannot verify, materials it cannot account for and paperwork it does not have. Every unknown goes into the price as risk.

An incoming contractor prices more keenly when it receives:

- **A dated photographic and written record** of the works in place, including concealed work recorded before it was covered, where it exists.
- **The consultant's technical report** on progress and conformity.
- **A defects list**, so the new contractor is not later blamed for, or paid twice for, the previous contractor's work.
- **An inventory of materials and equipment on site**, with ownership confirmed.
- **The current drawings, specification and outstanding design information**, with nothing left open that can be closed.
- **A clear scope to complete**, separating remedial work from new work.

Without that pack, a recovery price carries every doubt as contingency. With it, the job can usually restart at a fair price.

## What mistakes make a stalled villa worse?

- Paying an advance or a "goodwill" sum to get labour back, with no change to the programme or the terms.
- Terminating by email in frustration, before checking notice provisions and taking legal advice.
- Letting another builder start work before the change is approved and a new permit is in place.
- Allowing the outgoing contractor to remove materials or equipment without an agreed inventory.
- Letting the bond, the insurance or the defects record lapse while attention is on the argument.
- Agreeing a new price for "finishing" without separating remedial work from incomplete work.

Most of these come from the same instinct: wanting the site busy again quickly. A week spent establishing the facts usually saves months later.
