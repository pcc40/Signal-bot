Taking the keys to a new villa from a developer feels like the end of the process. For the condition of the house, it is closer to the beginning. Once you accept the villa, everything you find afterwards has to be chased through the developer's service request process, and some of it will be argued about.

This guide is for owners buying a completed or off-plan villa from a Dubai developer. If you are building your own villa with your own contractor, the process and your leverage are different; our [new villa snagging checklist](/insights/villa-snagging-checklist-dubai/) covers that case.

## Why is a developer handover different?

On a private build, you appoint the contractor, the contract sets the handover process, and you usually hold retention and a final payment until the snags are closed. On a developer villa, none of that applies:

- You did not appoint the contractor and you have no contract with it. Your agreement is with the developer.
- There is usually no retention. You will normally have paid the final instalment and service charges before you can take possession.
- The developer's process sets the order. Emaar's 2025 handover fact sheet, for example, lists settling outstanding payments, receiving a certificate of completion of payment, paying the DEWA security deposit, completing land registration and then attending handover.

That leaves you with two kinds of leverage: the inspection before you accept the villa, and the written record you keep afterwards. Both need to be done properly.

## What does the law and the developer cover?

There are two layers, and they are often confused.

| Cover | What it applies to | How long | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Developer's statutory liability (structure) | Structural parts of jointly owned property | 10 years from the completion certificate | Dubai Law No. 6 of 2019, Article 40 |
| Developer's statutory liability (installations) | Installations such as electrical, plumbing and mechanical | 1 year from handover | Dubai Law No. 6 of 2019, Article 40 |
| Developer's defects period (example: Emaar) | Electrical, plumbing and cooling systems; cladding; caulking, windows and doors where water gets in | 1 year from snag completion or the contract completion date, whichever is later | Emaar Community Management DLP factsheet |

Each developer runs its own defects process, so read yours. The Emaar factsheet also lists exclusions, including work supplied by the purchaser or its contractors, normal wear and tear, improper maintenance or misuse, drain blockages not caused by construction debris, cosmetic damage after orientation, and items not reported in writing within the warranty time frames. That last exclusion is the one owners most often fall foul of.

Take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer on how these rules apply to your villa and your sale and purchase agreement.

## The 20 items we check first

From the contractor's side, I know which parts of a house get finished last and checked least: the ones behind a ceiling, under a tile or on the roof. A snag list full of paint marks and missing silicone can look thorough while missing the problems that cost real money. So we start with the systems and the building envelope, then move to finishes.

| # | Item | What we do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Roof and terrace waterproofing | Look at falls, outlets and upstands; water test where allowed | Leaks show months later, inside |
| 2 | Wet room floors | Flood test showers and check falls to drains | Water under tiles damages the room below |
| 3 | Windows and doors | Check seals, drainage slots, locking and alignment; hose test where allowed | Water and dust ingress, AC losses |
| 4 | External walls and cladding | Look for cracks, open joints and failed sealant | The developer cover names cladding and water entry |
| 5 | Air conditioning | Run every unit; check supply temperature, noise and condensate drainage | The most expensive system to get wrong in Dubai |
| 6 | AC access | Confirm every unit, filter and valve can be reached for servicing | Poor access costs money every service |
| 7 | Hot water | Run hot water at every outlet; check heater, pressure and recovery time | Faults show only under use |
| 8 | Drainage | Run water at every outlet and check for slow drains and smells | Blockages from construction debris are covered; later ones may not be |
| 9 | Electrical distribution board | Check labelling, test the residual current devices, look for heat | Safety, and evidence for later claims |
| 10 | Sockets and lighting | Test every socket, switch and fitting | Quick to do and easy to forget |
| 11 | Moisture readings | Check walls and floors near wet rooms, roofs and external doors | Finds leaks before they stain |
| 12 | Thermal imaging | Look for missing insulation, hot spots and hidden leaks | Shows what the eye cannot |
| 13 | Pool (if fitted) | Run the plant, check for leaks, test lighting and safety features | Pool plant faults are costly to trace |
| 14 | Kitchen | Test every appliance, check worktop joints, sealing and extraction | Warranties start at handover |
| 15 | Joinery and wardrobes | Check doors, hinges, alignment and soft-close fittings | Easier to fix before you move in |
| 16 | Floor finishes | Look for hollow tiles, lippage, cracks and damaged stone | Hollow tiles can crack later |
| 17 | Walls and ceilings | Check for cracks, uneven finishes and patched areas | Patches can hide services work |
| 18 | External areas | Check paving falls, boundary walls, gates and garden irrigation | Ponding water and failed gates are common |
| 19 | Smart home and security | Test access control, cameras, intercom and any home automation | Systems often need setting up and handing over |
| 20 | Paperwork | Collect warranties, drawings and manuals; check meter readings | Your evidence if you need to claim later |

On a villa, items 1 to 12 usually matter more than the rest put together.

## What paperwork should you collect?

Emaar's fact sheet says a villa handover includes keys, a USB with warranty details, drawings, community rules and important contact numbers. Whatever your developer provides, make sure you leave with:

- the keys and access cards for every door and gate, counted and listed;
- warranties for appliances, AC and any pool or specialist equipment;
- drawings, ideally showing services routes, which you will need for any later renovation;
- operation and maintenance information for the AC, water heating, pool and smart systems;
- meter readings on the day, photographed;
- your snag list, signed or acknowledged, with a reference number and an agreed date for each item.

If something is missing, write it on the list rather than accepting a promise to send it later.

## How do you keep the snag list moving?

Most snag lists stall after handover. In our experience, the ones that get closed share a few habits:

1. **Report everything in writing**, through the developer's service request route, and keep every reference number.
2. **Photograph each item** with a wide shot that shows where it is and a close-up that shows the defect.
3. **Agree dates** for each item and follow up when they pass.
4. **Re-inspect** after the developer says items are closed. Do not sign them off from a message.
5. **Diarise the end of the defects period** and do a full inspection a month or two before it, so anything new is reported in time.

Avoid starting your own alterations before the snags are closed. If your contractor works on an area with an open defect, the developer may say the problem is now yours.
