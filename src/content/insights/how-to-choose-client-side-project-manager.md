To choose a client-side project manager for a Dubai villa, test five things in this order: independence (who pays them, and whether anything comes from anyone else on your project), limits of authority, who actually attends site and how often, what you receive in reports, and how the fee is calculated. Ask every firm the same questions, in writing, against the same scope of works. A good firm will answer each one plainly; a vague answer is itself an answer.

This guide explains what to test, the conflicts to look out for, the questions to ask any firm, including us, and the red flags. If you are still deciding whether you need a client-side project manager at all, start with [owner's representative, project manager or consultant](/insights/owners-representative-vs-project-manager-vs-consultant/) and [what a client-side project manager is worth](/why-a-client-side-project-manager/).

## What does a client-side project manager do on a Dubai villa?

A client-side project manager (also called an owner's representative) acts for you, the owner, on cost, programme, contract and decisions. It sits beside two other appointments you make directly: your registered consultant, who designs the villa and carries the statutory technical role, and your contractor, who builds it.

That division matters in Dubai. Under Law No. 14 of 2025, nobody may practise engineering consultancy without authorisation, and individuals or offices may not present themselves as engineering consultancy offices without a valid licence; registered offices must record their licensed scope, classification and technical staff with Dubai Municipality, and fines run up to AED 100,000 ([Dubai Media Office](https://www.mediaoffice.ae/en/news/2025/october/05-10/mohammed-bin-rashid), [Law No. 14 of 2025](https://www.dm.gov.ae/documents/law-no-14-of-2025-regulating-engineering-consultancy-practice-in-the-emirate-of-dubai-municipality/)). A client-side project manager who is not a registered consultancy should say so clearly, and should never offer to stand in for your consultant.

## Is the project manager independent?

Independence is the first test because everything else depends on it. A project manager checks other people's work: the design against your budget, the contractor's price against the market, payment applications against progress. That only works if the checker has no stake in the answer.

The professional bodies are clear about this. The Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors (RICS) says a member "must not advise or represent a client" where doing so would involve a conflict of interest, unless everyone affected has given prior informed consent. It treats a conflict between a client's interests and the firm's own interests, an "own interest conflict", as carrying greater risk ([RICS, Conflicts of interest](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Conflicts%20of%20interest_global_July23%20(1).pdf)). The Project Management Institute's code requires project managers to "proactively and fully disclose any real or potential conflicts of interest" ([PMI Code of Ethics](https://www.pmi.org/-/media/pmi/documents/public/pdf/ethics/pmi-code-of-ethics.pdf)).

In practice, ask four questions:

- **Who pays you?** The answer should be "only you".
- **Do you receive anything from anyone else on the project?** That includes commissions, referral fees, rebates, discounts or hospitality from contractors, consultants, subcontractors or suppliers.
- **Do you, or any related company, design, build, supply or fit out?** If so, how is that kept apart from your project?
- **Will you put the answers in the appointment?** A promise in a meeting is not the same as a clause.

### Conflicts with design-and-build and contracting arms

Some firms offer project management alongside design, contracting or interiors, either in the same company or through a sister company. That is not wrong in itself, but it changes what you are buying. A firm cannot independently review a design it produced, a price it submitted or progress it is responsible for.

RICS makes a related point: in UK practice, a surveyor's appointment "is unlikely to include the roles of both designer and project manager", and there is "a clear distinction between the two" ([RICS GN 107/2013](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/appointing_a_project_manager_1st_edition_rics.pdf)). If you choose a firm with a design or contracting arm, ask how it would handle a recommendation that went against its own sister company, and who would make that call. We look at the trade-offs in more detail in [design and build versus a separate architect](/insights/design-build-vs-separate-architect-villa/).

How people are paid also shapes what they recommend. In August 2025, Dubai Municipality warned engineering consultancy offices that some villa structural designs went beyond the safety requirements of the Dubai Building Code and raised construction costs without justification ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/uae/dubai-municipality-warns-consultancies-inflating-villa-designs-raising-costs-1.500245704)). That warning concerned designers, not project managers, but the lesson carries: understand whether a higher build cost pays anyone you appoint more.

## What authority will the project manager have?

RICS lists "authority levels" among the elements every project manager's appointment should include, and says a project manager should "always act within the terms of their authority to avoid conflicts of interest and dispute" ([RICS GN 107/2013](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/appointing_a_project_manager_1st_edition_rics.pdf)). On a private villa, authority is where owners most often lose control without noticing.

Decide in writing which decisions stay with you. Our own [limits of authority](/fees/#limits) are simple: we never instruct the contractor or approve a payment without the owner's written approval. Whatever firm you choose, the table below is a useful starting point.

| Decision | Who should decide | What the project manager should do |
|---|---|---|
| Instructions to the contractor | You, or your consultant where the contract gives them that role | Advise, draft and track, but not issue without your written approval |
| Variations that change cost or time | You, in writing | Check the price and the time claimed, and recommend |
| Interim payments | You, on your consultant's checked figures | Check applications against progress and contract rates, and recommend |
| Choice of consultant, contractor and suppliers | You appoint | Help you compare bids like-for-like and choose |
| Design and technical decisions | Your registered consultant, with you | Raise cost, programme and buildability questions |

Watch for an appointment that gives the project manager open-ended power to "act as the owner's agent". It sounds convenient when you live abroad, but it removes the check you are paying for.

## Who will actually attend site, and how often?

A project management fee is mostly the cost of people's time, so the people are the service. In RICS's suggested criteria for assessing project management proposals, "key personnel" carries the largest weighting, 30% to 40% of the quality score ([RICS GN 107/2013](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/appointing_a_project_manager_1st_edition_rics.pdf)).

Ask for these in writing:

- The person who leads your project, by name, and what they personally do.
- Who covers when that person is away, and whether the lead can be replaced without your agreement.
- How often someone attends site at each stage, and which meetings they attend.
- Who attends the moments that matter: before concrete is poured, before walls and ceilings are closed, at commissioning and at the snagging walkround.

At Eastbridge, our founder, Philip Chapman, leads every appointment personally, supported by a team of three project managers who attend site and provide cover. Site attendance is agreed in writing in the scope of each appointment.

From the builder's side, we can tell you how this looks on the other side of the table. For 21 years we ran the contractor's site, and we knew within a month which client representatives would turn up unannounced and which would only read the monthly report. We prepared for the first kind. Questions asked on site, before something is covered up, change what gets built. Questions asked from a report change what gets argued about later.

## What reports should you expect?

Reporting is how you see the project when you are not there, which for many owners is most of the time. Ask for a sample report from a past appointment, with names removed, and check it answers four questions: where is the money, where is the programme, what has changed, and what do you need to decide.

The FIDIC White Book, the international standard form for appointing consultants, which FIDIC describes as suitable for services including project management ([FIDIC](https://fidic.org/node/42966)), treats reporting as an absolute obligation in its 2017 edition, alongside a programme the consultant must provide within 14 days of commencement ([Curtis](https://omanlawblog.curtis.com/2017/10/whats-new-in-2017-fidic-white-book.html)). Whatever form you use, the scope should say what reports you receive, how often, and what each contains: photographs, cost to date and forecast final cost, programme against plan, open variations, risks, and the decisions needed from you. ## How is the fee calculated?

Fee models are covered in detail in [client-side project manager fees in Dubai](/insights/client-side-project-manager-fees-dubai/). When choosing between firms, three points matter most:

- **What a percentage is applied to.** RICS says it is important to agree whether a percentage applies to the approved budget, the accepted tender or the final cost of the works ([RICS GN 107/2013](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/appointing_a_project_manager_1st_edition_rics.pdf)). On the final cost, the fee rises when the cost rises.
- **What is excluded.** Your registered consultant's fee, other specialists, travel, disputes and the defects period are commonly outside a project manager's fee.
- **What happens if the build overruns.** Agree how extra months and extra cost are priced before anything goes wrong.

Our published ranges are on our [fees page](/fees/): a review costs AED 9,500–15,000 per review, part service is a monthly fee agreed in the written scope, and full service is typically 1.75–3% of build cost. Dubai Municipality also publishes guidance contract forms for consultancy and contracting on private villas, in Arabic, which are worth reading beside any appointment you are offered ([Dubai Municipality](https://www.dm.gov.ae/municipality-business/guidance-contracts-forms-for-engineering-consulting-services-and-construction-contracting-for-private-villas/)).

## Questions to ask any client-side project manager, including us

1. Who pays you, and do you receive anything from anyone else on my project?
2. Do you or any related company design, build, supply or fit out?
3. Are you a registered engineering consultancy? If not, how will you work alongside mine?
4. Who leads my project, by name, and who covers for them?
5. How often will someone attend site at each stage, and what will I receive afterwards?
6. Can you instruct the contractor, agree a variation or release a payment without my written approval?
7. Is the fee fixed, monthly or a percentage, and if a percentage, of what?
8. What is excluded, and how is work outside the scope priced?
9. Can I see a sample monthly report?
10. How does the appointment end, and what do I receive at the end?

## Red flags when choosing a project manager

- **A vague answer about who pays them**, or a reluctance to put it in the appointment.
- **A preferred contractor list you are expected to use**, rather than help to compare the contractors you choose to invite.
- **An unnamed team**, or a senior person at the pitch who never appears again.
- **No stated site attendance**, or attendance described only as "as required".
- **Open-ended authority** to instruct, agree variations or release payments without asking you.
- **A percentage fee with no stated base** and no position on overruns.
- **An offer to replace your registered consultant**, or to take on roles the law reserves for registered offices.
- **No questions about you.** A good project manager asks about your budget, your timescale, how decisions are made in your family and how often you are in Dubai, before proposing anything.
