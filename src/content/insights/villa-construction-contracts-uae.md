A villa construction contract in the UAE protects you on variations and delays only if it says clearly how changes are instructed and priced, how delays are notified and assessed, and what happens if the villa is late, and if both sides then follow those rules every week of the build. Most disputes we have seen did not start with a bad contract. They started with a reasonable contract that nobody administered.

This article explains the main mechanisms in plain English. It is general information, not legal advice. UAE law governs how these clauses are interpreted and enforced, and you should take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer before signing or disputing a contract.

## Is there a standard villa construction contract in Dubai?

Dubai Municipality publishes two guidance contract forms for private villas: one for engineering consultancy services and one for construction contracting. DM's stated aim is to strengthen contractual relationships between owners, consultants and contractors and reduce the disputes that delay projects.

Three points matter:

- The forms are **currently available in Arabic only**.
- They are **for guidance and not mandatory**.
- The parties **may amend them** by agreement.

They are a sensible starting point, and the [forms can be downloaded from Dubai Municipality](https://www.dm.gov.ae/municipality-business/guidance-contracts-forms-for-engineering-consulting-services-and-construction-contracting-for-private-villas/). If you do not read Arabic, get a reliable translation and have any amendments reviewed by a UAE-qualified lawyer. The Arabic text will normally be the one that counts.

Your consultant's appointment matters too. Under Dubai Law No. 14 of 2025, engineering consultancy across Dubai, including free zones, is regulated, and only registered consultancies may practise as engineering consultants. Make sure the contract and the consultant's appointment fit together: who approves what, who values payments, who issues instructions.

## Lump sum or remeasure: which contract do I have?

This is the first thing to establish, because it decides who carries the risk of quantities.

| | Lump sum | Remeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Price basis | Fixed price for a defined scope | Agreed rates applied to measured quantities |
| Who carries quantity risk | Mainly the contractor | Mainly the owner |
| Certainty of final cost | Higher, if the scope is complete | Lower until the work is measured |
| What drives extra cost | Variations to the defined scope | Changes in quantities and variations |
| Best suited to | Complete drawings and specification | Work that cannot be fully defined at the start |

A lump sum is only as fixed as the scope behind it. If the drawings are incomplete, the "fixed" price will move through variations. Many villa contracts are a blend: a lump sum for most of the work, with remeasurable elements such as excavation or piling, plus [provisional sums](/glossary/#provisional-sum). Know which items sit where.

## How should payments be valued and approved?

Payments should follow work done, not the calendar. A sound process looks like this:

1. The contractor submits a monthly application showing work completed and materials on site.
2. The owner's consultant checks it against progress on site and the contract rates.
3. The approved amount is issued, less retention and any agreed deductions.
4. The owner pays within the period the contract sets.

Owners get into trouble when they pay against a contractor's application without it being checked, or when the payment schedule is front-loaded so that the contractor is paid ahead of the work. Once you have overpaid, your leverage on quality and completion is gone.

## Retention, advance payments and performance bonds

These are the owner's main forms of financial security.

**Retention** is a percentage held back from each payment. Typically part is released at completion and the balance at the end of the defects liability period. It gives the contractor a reason to return and fix defects.

**Advance payment guarantee.** If you pay a mobilisation advance, it should be secured by a bank guarantee for the same amount, reducing as the advance is recovered from later payments.

**Performance bond.** A bank guarantee, often a percentage of the contract value, that the owner can call on if the contractor fails to perform. Check its wording, its expiry date and the conditions for calling it. A bond that expires before completion protects no one.

Ask also for evidence of contractors' all-risks and third-party liability insurance for the duration of the works.

## How do variations work in a villa contract?

A variation is any change to the work described in the contract: an added room, a different finish, a moved wall, an omission. Variations are normal. Unmanaged variations are the single biggest cause of budget overruns we have seen.

A good variation procedure has four steps:

1. **Instruction in writing.** Only the person named in the contract can instruct a change, and only in writing.
2. **Quotation before work.** The contractor prices the change, including any effect on time, before carrying it out.
3. **Agreement.** The owner accepts, rejects or negotiates the cost and time.
4. **Record.** The agreed variation is logged and carried into the next payment and the running final account.

On the contractor's side, we learned early that verbal instructions were where margin was made or lost. A client who asked for "just a small change" on a site walk, without paper, would see it reappear later at a price and with a time claim attached. Insisting on written instructions protects both sides.

Keep a variation register: number, description, date instructed, quoted cost, agreed cost, time effect, status. Review it monthly against the budget.

## Extension of time and liquidated damages, in plain English

These two clauses work as a pair.

**Liquidated damages (LDs)** are a sum the contractor pays for each day or week the villa is late beyond the completion date. They are agreed in advance so the owner does not have to prove the actual loss of a late handover. How they are treated is a matter of UAE law, including whether a court or tribunal may adjust them, so take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer on your specific clause.

**Extension of time (EOT)** moves the completion date when the delay is caused by something the contract says is not the contractor's risk: owner changes, late information from the owner's side, authority delays outside the contractor's control, or other listed events. If an EOT is granted, the contractor is not liable for LDs for that period.

A well-drafted contract also sets out:

- **Notice periods.** The contractor must notify a delay event within a set time.
- **Substantiation.** The notice should be followed by details of cause, effect and evidence.
- **Assessment.** The person named in the contract assesses the claim within a set period.
- **Loss and expense.** Whether the contractor can recover additional cost, such as extended preliminaries, as well as time.

From two decades of preparing and answering EOT claims under JCT contracts and through adjudication, our view is that the outcome is decided by the programme and the records, not the rhetoric. A claim supported by an updated programme, dated correspondence and site records is hard to resist. A rejection with no evidence behind it is equally hard to defend.

## What does good contract administration look like?

This is where we draw most directly on two decades on the builder's side. The projects that ended well shared the same habits:

- **A baseline programme agreed at the start**, updated monthly, with the critical path shown.
- **Weekly site records**: labour and plant on site, work done, weather, instructions received, problems encountered, with photographs.
- **Minuted progress meetings** with actions and owners, issued within days.
- **One instruction route.** All changes in writing through the named person.
- **A live variation register and cost report**, reviewed against budget every month.
- **Notices dealt with on time**, by both sides, every time.
- **Correspondence filed** so it can be found a year later.

None of this is glamorous. All of it decides who wins an argument about time and money.

## How are villa construction disputes resolved?

In general terms, most contracts provide for a sequence: negotiation between the parties, sometimes escalation to senior representatives or mediation, then a binding route such as arbitration or the courts, depending on what the contract says. The UK statutory adjudication regime that many British owners know does not apply in the same way in the UAE. The right route, the time limits and the costs depend on your contract and on UAE law, so take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer early. The cheapest dispute is the one prevented by good records. ## Before you sign: a short checklist

- Is the scope complete, and are all drawings and specifications listed in the contract?
- Is the price basis clear: lump sum, remeasure or a blend?
- Are provisional sums and exclusions listed and understood?
- Is there a written variation procedure with pricing before work?
- Are notice periods for delays and claims stated?
- Are LDs, retention, the advance payment guarantee and performance bond defined, with dates?
- Does the payment process tie to checked work on site?
- Has a UAE-qualified lawyer reviewed it?
