You can often live in your villa while it is renovated, but only if the work is planned around it from the start. The house has to be split into a part that is being worked on and a part that stays clean, cool and safe, and the contract has to say so. If the renovation includes rewiring, re-plumbing or replacing the air conditioning throughout, most families are better off moving out for at least part of the job.

This guide sets out how to decide, what the contractor needs to know before pricing, and the rules that shape the working day in Dubai.

## Should you stay or move out?

The answer depends less on the size of the budget than on which systems the work touches. A kitchen and two bathrooms in a five-bedroom villa is a very different proposition from a full services replacement in the same house.

| Type of work | Staying in | What usually decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Kitchen only | Usually workable | A temporary kitchen elsewhere in the house, and a clear route for deliveries |
| One or two bathrooms | Usually workable | Another bathroom that stays in use, and isolating the water only for short, agreed periods |
| Flooring throughout | Hard | Every room is affected in turn, furniture has to move, and adhesives and finishes need ventilation |
| Rewiring throughout | Hard | Power off in sections, ceilings and walls opened in occupied rooms |
| Re-plumbing throughout | Hard | Water off in sections, wet rooms out of use, ceilings opened to reach pipework |
| Air conditioning replacement | Very hard in summer | Cooling lost zone by zone; in July and August that can make rooms unusable |
| Extension or structural work | Depends on the connection | Living alongside is possible until the new part is broken through into the old |
| External works, pool, landscaping | Usually workable | Access, noise and keeping children away from open excavations |

Families with babies, older relatives or anyone with asthma or a respiratory condition should take the dust and noise more seriously than this table suggests. The US Environmental Protection Agency's guidance for home remodelling says to keep occupants, especially children, away from the work area and to clean it before they return.

## What does staying in add to the price?

Owners usually think of staying in as a saving: no rent, no second move. That is true, but it is not free.

From the contractor's side, the first question we asked on any renovation was whether the client would be living in. If they were, the price went up, and it should have. A team working around a family loses time every day. It sets up and packs away protection, keeps a route through the house clear, stops for the school run, waits while a room is vacated, and cleans more thoroughly at the end of every day. The programme stretches because work that could run in parallel has to be done in sequence, one zone at a time.

A good contractor prices that honestly. A less careful one prices the job as if the house were empty, then discovers the cost on site and looks for it elsewhere, usually in variations. Before you sign:

- Tell every bidder in writing that you will be living in, and which rooms you need throughout.
- Ask for the cost of working around occupation as its own line, so you can compare it between bids and against the cost of renting.
- Ask for a programme that shows the phases, not just a start and finish date.

Then compare like with like: the cost and length of the job with you in, against the cost of renting plus a shorter, simpler job. Our guide to [planning a villa renovation in Dubai](/insights/how-to-plan-a-villa-renovation-dubai/) covers the earlier decisions that make this comparison possible.

## How do you phase a renovation around a family?

Phasing means agreeing, before work starts, which parts of the house are handed to the contractor and when, and which stay with you. It works best when the phases follow the services, not just the rooms.

1. **Agree the living zone.** Usually a bedroom suite, one bathroom, a place to prepare food and a route in and out that does not cross the work.
2. **Separate the services.** Check whether the living zone can keep power, water and cooling while the rest of the house is isolated. In older villas it often cannot without temporary works, and those cost money.
3. **Sequence the dirty work first.** Demolition, chasing walls and cutting tiles create the most dust. Do as much as possible in one concentrated period, ideally when you are away.
4. **Set a separate access.** The EPA suggests that for high-dust jobs workers use an entrance separate from the occupants until clean-up is complete. In a villa, that often means a side gate and a garden door, not the front door.
5. **Plan the handover of each zone.** Before you move back into a finished area, inspect it, test what has been installed, and agree what is left.

Our guide to [rewiring and replumbing an older villa](/insights/older-villa-mep-renovation/) explains why services work in particular is hard to phase.

## How do you keep the dust and the AC under control?

Dust is the complaint we heard most often from clients who stayed in, and in Dubai the air conditioning makes it worse. A ducted system on in one part of the house can draw dust from the work area through the returns and spread it everywhere else, and once it is in the ducts it keeps coming out.

The EPA's remodelling guidance is practical and applies directly:

- seal the work area with plastic sheeting taped over doorways and openings;
- tape plastic over the supply and return grilles in the room being worked on, so dust does not spread or settle in the ducts;
- use low-dust methods, such as misting before sanding or cutting;
- extract air out of the work area to the outside rather than letting it drift into the house;
- after new finishes or flooring go down, ventilate as much as possible during installation and for at least 72 hours afterwards.

Add the Dubai-specific points to the contract as well:

- **Which AC zones stay on.** Name them. If a unit or zone has to be switched off, agree when and for how long.
- **Filters.** The contractor changes the filters on any unit serving the living zone during the works and again at the end.
- **Duct cleaning.** Where ducts were exposed to dust despite the protection, cleaning them before you move back is part of the job, not an extra.
- **Summer.** If the work includes replacing units, avoid July and August unless you are away.

## What are the working hours in Dubai?

According to Gulf News, citing Dubai Municipality, construction work is permitted from 6am to 8pm on weekdays and 7am to 8pm at weekends, with a daytime noise limit of 55 decibels. Master developers can set stricter rules for their own communities, so read your community's guidelines and the conditions on your modification approval, and write the agreed hours into the contract. If you are living in, you may want later starts or quieter mornings than the rules allow, and the contractor will price that too.

The summer midday break also shapes the programme. In 2026, the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation banned outdoor work in direct sunlight between 12:30pm and 3pm from 15 June to 15 September, as it has every summer for more than two decades. Indoor work can continue, but roofing, external works, the pool and landscaping all slow down. ## What about insurance?

There are two policies to think about: yours and the contractor's.

**Your home policy.** Read it before work starts and speak to your insurer. One UAE home policy we reviewed, distributed through a bank, requires the policyholder to report changes to the information given when they applied. It also excludes water damage through an opening made for alterations or renovations, excludes damage caused by renovating, and excludes subsidence damage while the property is undergoing structural alteration. If you move out, the same policy excludes most loss after 60 consecutive days of the home being unoccupied. Other policies will differ, so ask yours directly what is covered while the builders are in, and get the answer in writing.

**The contractor's policy.** Dubai's Executive Council Resolution No. 28 of 2022 requires a contractor to provide insurance covering the works and third-party liability to obtain a permit for building construction, for alteration or addition works of 300 square metres or more, and for demolition and shoring. Many villa renovations are smaller than that, so the law may not require the cover. Ask for it anyway, see the certificates before work starts, and check that the policy covers your existing house and contents, not just the new work. Take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer or insurance broker on the wording.

## How do you protect what stays?

Every renovation we built had a moment when a finished floor, a door frame or a piece of furniture was damaged by a trade that did not own it. In an occupied house, there is more of the client's property in the way.

- **Agree what the contractor protects.** Floors on the route through the house, stair treads, door frames, glazing and any built-in joinery that stays.
- **Move or store what can be moved.** A storage unit for good furniture and art is often cheaper than the arguments afterwards.
- **Photograph the existing condition.** Walk the house with the contractor before work starts and record what is already marked or damaged. It settles questions later.
- **Security.** Agree who holds keys, how the house is locked at the end of each day, and which rooms are out of bounds to the workforce.

## When moving out is the better answer

In our experience, a family should seriously consider moving out when:

- the work replaces the electrics, plumbing or air conditioning throughout;
- the programme runs through July and August and includes cooling work;
- someone in the household has a respiratory condition, is very young or is elderly;
- the house is the owner's main place of work as well as home.

A shorter programme in an empty house, with the contractor's full team on site, can cost less overall than a longer one built around the family. Our [villa renovation timeline guide](/insights/villa-renovation-timeline-dubai/) shows how the stages fit together.
