An independent contractor quote review is a check of a villa contractor's price, scope, programme and payment terms, carried out for the owner, by someone the contractor does not pay, before the contract is signed. In Dubai and Abu Dhabi it usually means putting every bid into one like-for-like table, testing the provisional sums, PC sums, exclusions and qualifications, and giving the owner a written report with the questions to ask before signing. At Eastbridge it is priced within our Review range, AED 9,500–15,000 per review, and agreed in writing before we start.

This guide explains what the review covers, step by step, and what you should receive at the end, whoever carries it out.

## Why have a villa quote reviewed before you sign?

Because the contract sum is not the final cost. A villa quote is a contractor's reading of your drawings and specification, and every gap in them is priced somewhere: as an allowance, an exclusion, an assumption, or nothing at all and a variation later.

Our founder, Philip Chapman, ran P Chapman Construction, a premium UK residential builder, for 21 years, and priced quotes like these himself. On the contractor's side, the first thing we did with any set of tender drawings was list what they did not say. Those lines decided whether a job made money. An owner who has the same list before signing negotiates from a very different position.

A review is most useful at two points: when you have two or more bids to choose between, and when you have one preferred contractor and a draft contract in front of you.

## Step 1: are the quotes priced on the same information?

Before comparing any numbers, check that every bidder priced the same thing. RICS guidance on tendering says the analysis of returned tenders "must be carried out methodically and thoroughly to ensure parity for all tenderers and to ensure tender returns are reviewed on a like-for-like basis" ([RICS, Tendering strategies](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/tendering_strategies_1st_edition_rics.pdf)).

On a private villa, the most common reasons bids are not like-for-like are simple:

- Different drawing revisions were sent to different contractors, or one bidder priced from an earlier set.
- One contractor priced a [bill of quantities](/glossary/#boq) and another priced from drawings and specification, measuring the quantities themselves.
- One quote includes the pool, landscaping, boundary walls or external works; another leaves them out without saying so clearly.

A bill of quantities, prepared under the RICS measurement rules for building works (NRM 2), gives every bidder the same quantities to price, which is why it makes bids easier to compare ([RICS NRM 2](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/october_2021_nrm_2.pdf)). Many private villas in Dubai are tendered on drawings and specification instead. That is workable, but the review then has to do more of the levelling.

We also check each bidder's registration. The Dubai BPS app, relaunched by Dubai Municipality in 2024, includes a registry of consultants and contractors ([Dubai Municipality](https://www.dm.gov.ae/new-improved-building-permits-app-launched-for-streamlined-services/)).

## Step 2: building a like-for-like bid table

The core of the review is a single table: every bid broken into the same sections, with the differences made visible. RICS describes the first step as a full arithmetical check of the pricing and a logic check of the rest, before any detailed analysis.

| Section of the bid table | What we check | Why it moves the comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Arithmetic | Totals, carried-forward figures, rates times quantities | Errors happen, and the tender rules decide whether a contractor may correct them or must stand by the price |
| Preliminaries | Site staff, set-up, welfare, insurances, temporary works, programme length | Turner & Townsend puts preliminaries in Dubai at 12% of construction cost on a 2,500 m² project; a thin figure often means a short programme or missing items |
| Measured work by element | Substructure, frame, envelope, MEP, finishes, external works | Shows where one bid is light or heavy against the others |
| Provisional and PC sums | Number, size and description of each allowance | Shows how much of the price is still an estimate |
| Exclusions and qualifications | Everything the contractor has left out or made conditional | Each one is a cost the owner will meet later |
| Programme | Start, completion, key dates, summer working assumptions | A shorter programme may not be achievable; a longer one costs more preliminaries |
| Payment terms | Advance, stage payments, retention, release dates | Decides how much money is at risk at each point |

Where a bid omits an item that others priced, RICS describes the methods a quantity surveyor uses to normalise it, such as using the average of the submitted costs, the highest price returned, or the cost plan allowance for that element. We say in the report which method we used, so you can see how the adjusted figures were reached. The preliminaries benchmark above is from [Turner & Townsend's 2025 UAE market intelligence](https://marketintelligence.turnerandtownsend.com/uaemi-2025/construction-cost-performance/).

## Step 3: provisional sums and PC sums

These two lines are where a fixed-looking price is least fixed.

A **[provisional sum](/glossary/#provisional-sum)** is an allowance for work that is not yet designed or defined in enough detail to price. NRM 2 distinguishes defined provisional sums, described well enough for the contractor to allow for them in programme and preliminaries, from undefined ones, where the owner carries the price and programme risk ([Gowling WLG](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/articles/2015/back-to-basics-provisional-sums/)). As Gowling WLG puts it: "The price of works detailed by provisional sums is indeed provisional."

A **[prime cost (PC) sum](/glossary/#prime-cost-sum)** is an allowance for an item still to be chosen, often from a supplier the owner selects: sanitaryware, a kitchen, lighting, ironmongery. When the real cost is known, the contract sum goes up or down by the difference ([Designing Buildings Wiki](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/The_difference_between_a_prime_cost_sum_and_a_provisional_sum)).

What we check:

- **Total value.** Add every provisional and PC sum together and express it as a share of the contract sum. In our experience, a large share on a villa that is meant to be fully designed is a warning sign.
- **Realism.** An allowance of a round figure for a stone floor or a kitchen that you have already chosen, and which will cost much more, flatters the bid. We compare each allowance with what you actually intend to buy.
- **Mark-up and attendance.** How the contractor will add overheads, profit and attendance to the final cost of each sum.
- **Time.** Whether the contract links an expanded provisional sum to an extension of time. Gowling WLG notes that some standard forms do not clearly deal with this.

## Step 4: exclusions, qualifications and assumptions

Read these pages as carefully as the price. An exclusion is something the contractor has not priced; a qualification is a condition attached to the price; an assumption is what the contractor believes about your project. On villa quotes we often see:

- MEP elements excluded or priced only to "first fix".
- Pool, landscaping, external lighting, gates or boundary walls excluded.
- Authority, master developer and utility connection fees excluded, or included only "where applicable".
- Rock, groundwater or dewatering excluded, or priced on an assumption about ground conditions.
- Prices valid for a short period, or subject to material price changes.
- VAT shown separately. UAE VAT is charged at 5% on taxable supplies unless an exemption or zero rate applies ([Federal Tax Authority](https://tax.gov.ae/DataFolder/Files/Pdf/VAT-Decree-Law-No-8-of-2017.pdf)); make sure every bid is compared on the same basis.

RICS warns about qualifications that would make a tender unacceptable, and recommends that clarification questions are worded so that bidders cannot use them to resubmit a different price. We draft those questions for you.

## Step 5: programme and payment terms

A cheaper bid with a payment schedule that runs ahead of the work can cost an owner more than a dearer one with fair terms, if anything goes wrong.

We check:

- **The programme.** Is the duration realistic for the design and the plot, including summer working limits and authority inspections? Does it match the preliminaries priced?
- **The advance payment.** How large it is, and whether it is protected by an [advance payment guarantee](/glossary/#advance-payment-guarantee).
- **Stage payments.** Whether payments follow measured work done and checked on site, or calendar dates and loosely defined milestones. Our [stage payment planner](/tools/stage-payment-planner/) shows how a schedule builds up.
- **Retention.** How much is held back and when it is released. RICS describes the principle: part released when the works are complete and the rest when the defects period has ended ([RICS GN 90/2012](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/retention_1st_edition_rics.pdf)). Commentary on UAE practice gives the same pattern under the FIDIC forms ([Kayrouz & Associates](https://www.kayrouzandassociates.com/insights/retention-final-account-uae-construction)).
- **Variations and delay.** How changes are priced and instructed, and the delay damages if the contractor finishes late.

Dubai Municipality publishes guidance contract forms for consultancy and contracting on private villas, in Arabic ([Dubai Municipality](https://www.dm.gov.ae/municipality-business/guidance-contracts-forms-for-engineering-consulting-services-and-construction-contracting-for-private-villas/)). They are a useful reference point when a contractor proposes its own terms. For the legal effect of any contract, take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer.

## What are the red flags in a villa contractor's quote?

None of these means a bid is dishonest. Each means the price is less certain than it looks.

- A lump sum with no breakdown, or a breakdown that does not add up.
- Preliminaries far below the other bids, with the same or a shorter programme.
- Large provisional or PC sums on items that are already designed or chosen.
- A long exclusions list, or exclusions written in general terms ("anything not specifically mentioned").
- A front-loaded payment schedule, or a large advance with no guarantee.
- A programme that ignores authority inspections or summer working limits.
- Rates for variations that are not stated, or stated only as "to be agreed".
- A price valid for a few days, used to push for a quick signature.

## What does the owner receive from a quote review?

You should receive a written report you can act on before signing. Ours contains:

- The like-for-like bid table, with the adjustments we made and why.
- For each bid, the scope gaps, provisional and PC sums, exclusions and qualifications, with our view of their likely cost effect.
- The programme and payment terms compared, with the points we would change.
- A list of questions to put to each contractor, worded so the answers can be compared.
- Our recommendation, with reasons. You decide and you appoint the contractor directly.

The review is a commercial check, not a design review: design and the statutory engineering role stay with your registered consultant. We work alongside your consultant, and we never instruct the contractor or commit you to anything without your written approval.

| What a review is | What it is not |
|---|---|
| An independent commercial check of price, scope, programme and payment terms | Legal advice on the contract |
| A like-for-like comparison of the bids you hold | A design review or a check of structural calculations |
| Questions and a recommendation, with reasons | A decision made for you: you choose and appoint |
