An owner building a villa in Dubai or Abu Dhabi should be on site, or have someone on their side on site, at six moments: setting out, before each major concrete pour, first fix, before walls and ceilings are closed, the pre-handover walk, and the end of the defects period. Everything else is useful but optional. These six are the points where a problem is still cheap to fix and where the evidence is still visible.

This guide explains what happens on site at each stage, what to look at, and how to use the visits to control payments. It is written from the builder's side: for 21 years our founder, Philip Chapman, ran a residential construction company in England, and these are the moments that the contractor knows matter.

## What does the owner's site visit actually do?

It is worth being clear about what an owner's visit is not. Your registered consultant is responsible for supervising the works under the building permit, and Dubai Municipality's procedures provide for staged inspections, for example of foundations and slabs, and a joint completion inspection before the completion certificate is issued ([Dubai Municipality](https://dmpmedia.dm.gov.ae/uploads/2020/06/Residential-Buildings_Building-Permits-Procedures.pdf)). In Abu Dhabi the municipality's own inspections follow the permit issued through TAMM. Those checks are about compliance with the approved drawings and the regulations.

The owner's visit answers different questions:

- Is the work that the contractor is asking to be paid for actually there?
- Is it being built to the specification you agreed, not a cheaper equivalent?
- Are decisions waiting on you, and is the programme slipping because of them?
- Does what is being built match what you thought you were getting?

No inspection regime answers those for you. That is why the visits are worth making, and why the timing matters.

## The six visits that matter most

| Visit | When | What you are checking | Why it cannot wait |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Setting out | Before excavation or foundations start | Position of the house on the plot, levels, setbacks, orientation of key rooms and views | Once foundations are in, the house cannot move |
| 2. Before major pours | Before each foundation, slab or roof pour | Openings, sleeves and services routes cast into the concrete; changes since the drawings | Concrete hides mistakes permanently |
| 3. First fix | After services are run, before plastering | Socket, switch and light positions; AC routes; data, AV and security containment; plumbing positions | Moving a socket now costs minutes; later it costs a wall |
| 4. Before close-up | Before ceilings are boarded and walls finished | Insulation, waterproofing at wet areas and terraces, fire stopping, access panels for valves and AC units | The last chance to see what will be hidden for 20 years |
| 5. Pre-handover | When the contractor says the work is complete | Snagging, testing and commissioning, documents and manuals | Your leverage drops sharply once you accept handover |
| 6. End of defects period | Before retention is released | Defects that appeared in use, outstanding snags | Retention is your last lever |

### 1. Setting out: the house on the plot

Before anything is dug, the contractor's surveyor sets out the building on the plot: the corners of the house, the levels and the positions of boundary walls and the pool. It looks like string lines and pegs, but it fixes where your house will sit for its whole life.

Walk the plot with the drawings. Stand where the living room will be and look at what you will see. Check the finished floor level against the street and the neighbours, because a house set too low collects water and a house set too high looks wrong on the street. If anything surprises you, ask now. On the contractor's side, a setting-out query was the cheapest question an owner ever asked us.

### 2. Before the major pours

Every slab, foundation and concrete roof has things cast into it: sleeves for drainage, openings for stairs and lifts, ducts for services. Once the concrete is poured, adding one means coring through structure, which needs the structural designer's agreement and sometimes is not possible at all.

Your consultant will check the reinforcement and the authority may inspect. Your own visit is about function: is there an opening where you expect your stair, your lift or your large rooflight? Has a change you agreed last month been carried through to the slab? Visit before the pour, not on the day, so there is time to correct anything.

In summer, pours are often scheduled early in the morning. The UAE midday break bans outdoor work under direct sunlight from 12:30pm to 3pm between 15 June and 15 September, although concrete pours that cannot be interrupted can be exempt ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/uae-midday-break-starts-june-15-work-under-direct-sunlight-banned-during-peak-afternoon-hours?amp=1)). Ask the site manager for the pour programme a week ahead.

### 3. First fix: where everything plugs in

First fix is when electricians, plumbers and AC installers run their cables, pipes and ducts before walls are plastered. It is the visit owners most often skip and most often regret.

Take the furniture layout with you. Stand at the bed and check the switches. Check where the television, the kitchen island, the desk and the outdoor lights go. Check that containment for data, AV and security systems is in, even if the equipment comes later. If you have an interior designer, bring them. In our experience, the single most common source of small, irritating variations on a finished house is a socket or a switch in the wrong place, and every one of them could have been moved for nothing at first fix.

### 4. Before close-up: the hidden work

This is the most important visit on the build. Before ceilings are boarded and walls finished, you can still see:

- waterproofing on wet rooms, terraces, balconies and roofs, ideally after a flood test;
- insulation in roofs and walls, which matters a great deal in a Dubai summer;
- fire stopping around services that pass between rooms;
- access to valves, AC units and drainage cleaning points, which should be reachable without breaking a finished ceiling.

On the contractor's side, the pressure is always to close up quickly, because finishes are what the client sees and what the programme is judged on. An owner, or someone on the owner's side, who asks to see the work before it is covered is not being difficult. They are doing the one check that cannot be done later.

### 5. Pre-handover: the snagging walk

When the contractor declares the work complete, walk the whole house with a written snag list, and ask for the testing and commissioning records for air conditioning, electrics, plumbing, the pool and any smart-home systems. Do not accept handover, or release the payment linked to it, until the list has been worked through. Our [villa snagging checklist](/insights/villa-snagging-checklist-dubai/) sets out what to check room by room and which documents to collect.

### 6. End of the defects period

Most villa contracts include a defects liability period after handover, during which the contractor must return to fix defects. Keep a log from the day you move in, and walk the house again before the period ends and before any retention is released. Our guide to the [defects liability period](/insights/defects-liability-period-uae/) explains how to keep your leverage through the first year.

## How to tie visits to payments

The most effective control an owner has is simple: the contractor is not paid for a stage until someone on the owner's side has seen it on site.

Most contractors submit a monthly payment application claiming a value for the work done. Your consultant assesses it under the contract. Alongside that, ask for each application to come with dated photographs of the work claimed, and line up your visits so that the larger stages, such as structure complete, roof complete and first fix complete, are seen before they are paid.

On the contractor's side, we knew which owners checked and which did not. A payment application to an owner who visits is a more careful document.

## If you live abroad

Many owners building here do not live in the UAE, and a villa build lasts long enough that monthly flights are not realistic. What matters is that the six key visits happen, and that whoever attends is working for you.

- **Agree the visit schedule in the contract**, with notice periods, so the contractor plans around it.
- **Ask for a written report after each visit**, with dated photographs taken from the same positions each time, so you can compare progress month to month.
- **Make the decisions log part of the report**: what is waiting for you, by when, and what it will cost in time if it is late.
- **Use video calls for first fix and close-up** if you cannot be there, walking the house room by room with the drawings on screen.

## What not to do on site

- **Do not instruct workers directly.** Raise points with your consultant or the site manager and confirm them in writing. A verbal instruction to a foreman can become a variation with no agreed price.
- **Do not approve changes on the spot.** Ask for the cost and the effect on the programme first.
- **Do not ignore the site rules.** Wear the protective equipment provided and follow the site manager's directions. A construction site is the contractor's responsibility while the works are under way.
