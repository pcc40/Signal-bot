An older villa in an established Dubai community can be the best-value way into a large house on a good plot. It can also come with twenty years of wear behind the paint. The listing photographs will not tell you which, and nor will a quick viewing on a bright afternoon.

This guide is for buyers of resale villas, particularly older ones, who want to know what they are buying before they commit. If you are taking a new villa from a developer, our guide to [snagging a developer villa before you accept it](/insights/snagging-developer-villa-handover/) covers that case.

## When should you survey?

Before you sign. A resale purchase in Dubai normally runs through Form F, the Dubai Land Department's memorandum of understanding between buyer and seller. Meraas describes Form F as setting the deadlines, transfer timing, handover terms and seller disclosures, with the deposit held in trust. Once it is signed, walking away usually has a cost.

That leaves two practical routes:

1. **Inspect before you sign.** Ask the seller for access for a survey once a price is agreed in principle.
2. **Make the purchase conditional.** Gulf News has described an inspection contingency clause: the buyer commissions an inspection, the report is delivered within an agreed number of days, and if the parties cannot agree how to deal with major issues, the deal falls away and the deposit is returned.

Either way, agree it in writing, and ask a UAE-qualified lawyer and your agent how the clause should be worded. Neither route works if the survey happens after the paperwork is fixed.

## What should you ask the seller first?

A survey is better when the inspector knows the history. Before you book one, ask the seller or their agent for:

- **The age of the villa** and the year of any major works since.
- **Maintenance records**, especially air conditioning servicing, pest control and any leak or drainage repairs.
- **Recent DEWA bills.** A high bill for the size of house can point to an inefficient AC system, poor insulation or a hidden leak.
- **Approvals and drawings** for every extension, pool, pergola or layout change.
- **Any warranties still running**, for example on a replaced AC system, roof waterproofing or pool equipment.
- **Whether the villa is tenanted**, which affects access for the survey and the condition you can expect.

Gaps in the answers are information too. A villa with no service history for the air conditioning should be surveyed on the assumption that it has not been serviced.

## Who should carry out the survey?

Choose a firm that does condition surveys of occupied houses, not only new-build snagging, and ask what equipment it uses: moisture meters, thermal imaging, drain cameras and electrical testing. Ask for a sample report. A useful report separates urgent safety items, expensive items and cosmetic items, and says plainly where the inspector could not see and what would need opening up to confirm. Agree the scope and the report date before you book, so the report arrives inside the window your sale agreement allows.

## What should the survey look at first?

From the builder's side, I learned that the expensive surprises in an old house are almost never in the finishes. They are in the things that were hidden when the house was new and have been ageing quietly since. That is where a survey of an older villa should start.

| Area | What to ask for | Why it matters on an older villa |
|---|---|---|
| Electrical installation | Age, condition of the distribution board, earthing, signs of overheating, any amateur alterations | Rewiring means opening walls and ceilings throughout |
| Water supply and plumbing | Pipe material, signs of leaks, water pressure, hot water | Old pipework fails from the inside |
| Drainage | Flow at every outlet, smells, a camera survey where problems are suspected | Blocked or broken drains under slabs are costly to reach |
| Air conditioning | Age of units, performance, ductwork condition, condensate drainage | Often the largest single replacement cost |
| Roof and terraces | Falls, outlets, waterproofing condition, previous patch repairs | Roof leaks damage the rooms below and the finishes in them |
| Wet rooms | Moisture readings around showers and baths, tiling condition | Water under tiles spreads before it shows |
| Structure | Cracks, movement, signs of steel corrosion or spalling concrete | Rare, but decisive if found |
| Windows and doors | Seals, glazing, frames, water and air leaks | Heat gain and dust in summer |
| External works | Pool, boundary walls, paving falls, irrigation | Pool plant and leaks are expensive to trace |
| Alterations | Extensions, enclosed terraces, converted rooms, pergolas | Approvals, quality of work and transfer risk |

Moisture readings and thermal imaging help a great deal in a finished house, because they show what the eye cannot. Our guide to [rewiring and replumbing an older villa](/insights/older-villa-mep-renovation/) explains what typically sits behind the walls and how contractors price it.

## What about opening-up?

A survey of a finished house can only see so much. Where the survey raises a question, the cheapest way to answer it is often to open up a few chosen places: lift a tile by a shower, look above a ceiling panel, open an access hatch to the roof void. The seller has to agree, and any opening-up has to be made good, so it needs arranging in advance.

The aim is not to strip the house. It is to turn the biggest unknowns into facts before you commit, so the price you agree reflects the house you are buying.

## Have the alterations been approved?

Many older villas have been extended or altered over the years: an enclosed terrace, a new majlis, a pool, a pergola, a converted garage, internal walls moved. Each of these needs approvals from the community or master developer and, depending on the work, the relevant authority.

Why it matters to a buyer:

- **Transfer.** In a case published by fäm Properties, a villa with an older pool needed new drawings and approvals from Dubai Municipality and the developer before the NOC for sale and transfer could be issued. The approvals took about four weeks.
- **Your future plans.** If you plan to renovate, the community will look at the villa as it stands. Unapproved work found then is yours to resolve.
- **Quality.** Work done without approval was also done without inspection, so it deserves a closer look in the survey.

Ask the seller for the approvals and drawings behind every alteration, and compare them with what is actually built. If they cannot be produced, take advice before you commit. Our guides to the [Nakheel NOC](/insights/nakheel-noc-villa-renovation/) and [renovating in an Emaar community](/insights/emaar-community-villa-renovation/) explain how approvals work in two of the largest master communities.

## What should you budget for after buying?

Even with a good survey, some things only show when work starts. Hold back an allowance for them, separate from the cost of any changes you want to make.

- **Base it on the survey.** Services near the end of their life should be budgeted as replacements, not repairs.
- **Separate wants from needs.** Price the essential work first: services, waterproofing and anything affecting safety. Then price the kitchen, bathrooms and finishes you want.
- **Use published ranges as a check.** Our [villa renovation cost guide](/insights/villa-renovation-cost-dubai/) sets out the figures, and the survey should tell you which parts of the house sit at the top of them.

If the survey suggests the structure, services and layout all need major work, it may be worth comparing renovation with rebuilding before you agree a price. Our [renovate or rebuild guide](/insights/renovate-or-rebuild-villa-dubai/) explains how to make that comparison fairly.
