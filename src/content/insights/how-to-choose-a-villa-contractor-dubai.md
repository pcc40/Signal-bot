To choose a villa contractor in Dubai, first confirm each bidder is properly registered using official sources, then compare prices only on the same drawings and specification, with preliminaries, provisional sums and exclusions set out side by side. After that, judge the people who will actually run your site, call recent clients, and agree payment terms that follow work done. The cheapest headline figure is rarely the cheapest villa.

We spent twenty-one years as the builder before moving to the client's side, so this guide is written from both ends of the table. The twelve questions below are the ones we would want answered before any owner signs.

## Before you ask anything: get the basis right

Contractor selection goes wrong most often before the first bid arrives. If bidders are pricing from incomplete drawings, a vague specification or different assumptions, you will receive numbers that cannot be compared.

Your registered consultant should provide a complete, consistent set of drawings and a specification. Under Dubai Law No. 14 of 2025, only offices with a valid trade licence and registration with Dubai Municipality may present themselves as engineering consultancy offices (Dubai Media Office), so check the consultant's registration as carefully as the contractor's. Every bidder prices exactly the same thing, on the same form, by the same date. The owner then appoints. Our role at Eastbridge is to help you compare the bids like-for-like and choose, alongside your consultant.

## The 12 questions to ask a villa contractor

### 1. Are you registered for this type of work, with this authority?

Do not take a logo on a website as proof. Dubai Municipality's relaunched BPS app includes a search for consultants and contractors registered in Dubai, and lets owners look at information such as a firm's current status and the type and number of projects it is handling. The Build in Dubai platform is the wider gateway for the permit process. Check the trade licence, its expiry date and its licensed activities: Dubai Economy and Tourism's online licence information service shows a mainland firm's licence status, expiry date, legal type and activities when you search by name or licence number (Khaleej Times). Then confirm the firm can work under the authority your plot falls within: Dubai Municipality, Trakhees or the Dubai Development Authority, depending on where your plot sits.

### 2. How many villas like ours are you building right now?

Workload matters as much as track record. A firm with too little work may be under financial pressure; a firm with too much will spread its best people thin. The BPS app's project information is a useful starting point for this conversation.

### 3. Who will run our site, and can we meet them?

The site manager or project manager who turns up every day decides the quality of your villa. Ask for names and CVs of the key staff, ask whether they will be dedicated to your project, and ask what happens if they leave. Make the named people a condition of the contract where you can.

On the contractor's side, we knew that the strongest site manager went to the job with the most demanding client and the best-run paperwork. That is not cynicism; it is how a builder protects its reputation with limited resources.

### 4. Is your price based on our drawings and specification, with nothing substituted?

Ask every bidder to confirm in writing that they have priced the documents as issued, and to list any alternatives separately. Substituted products and "equivalent" finishes buried in the pricing are a common way of shaving a headline number.

### 5. What is in your preliminaries?

Preliminaries are the cost of running the site rather than building the villa: site staff, welfare, hoarding, temporary power and water, insurance, scaffolding, plant, cleaning and waste. Turner & Townsend's 2025 UAE market intelligence puts preliminaries in Dubai at 12% of construction cost on a 2,500 m² project and 14% on a 50,000 m² project. A villa sits at the small end of that scale, so treat 12% as the reference point. A bid with preliminaries far below it is either very efficient or has moved costs elsewhere, or has left them out to be claimed later. Ask for a breakdown, and ask how the monthly figure changes if the programme extends.

### 6. What provisional sums have you included, and why?

A provisional sum is an allowance for work that cannot yet be fully defined. They are legitimate in small numbers. Large or numerous provisional sums mean the true price is still unknown. When comparing bids, list every provisional sum side by side and, where possible, set common figures for all bidders so the comparison is fair.

### 7. What have you excluded?

Read exclusions as carefully as the price. Pools, landscaping, external works, kitchens, wardrobes, lighting control, AV, authority fees and service connections are frequently excluded or qualified. Two bids with the same total can be several hundred thousand dirhams apart once exclusions are priced back in.

### 8. What is your programme, and what does it depend on?

Ask for a programme showing the main stages, the critical path and what the contractor needs from you and when: design information, selections, approvals, payments. A programme that looks fast but depends on you making every decision instantly is not a real programme. See [how long it takes to build a villa in Dubai](/insights/how-long-to-build-a-villa-dubai/) for typical durations.

### 9. Can we speak to your last three villa clients?

Not the three the contractor chooses to show off; the last three completed. And call them. Useful questions are: did the final account match the contract price, and if not, why? Was completion on time? How were defects handled after handover? Would you use them again? Whenever you can, visit a finished villa and a live site.

### 10. What payment terms are you asking for?

Avoid schedules that pay large sums upfront or against calendar dates. Payments should follow work actually completed and checked. Agree how each payment is valued, who checks it and how long you have to pay once it is agreed. Our article on [villa construction contracts in the UAE](/insights/villa-construction-contracts-uae/) covers how payments are valued in more detail.

### 11. What security will you provide: bonds, retention, insurance?

Ask whether the contractor will provide an advance payment guarantee against any mobilisation payment, a performance bond, and accept retention held back until completion and the end of the defects period. Ask for evidence of contractors' all-risks and third-party insurance. A contractor that resists all of these is telling you something about its balance sheet. Solvency matters beyond handover too: UAE law makes the contractor and the engineer jointly liable for ten years from handover for collapse or defects that threaten structural stability or safety, and that liability cannot be excluded by contract (Kayrouz & Associates). It is only as valuable as the firm standing behind it.

### 12. How do you handle changes and delays?

Every villa changes during construction. Ask how the contractor prices variations, how quickly it gives you a cost before carrying out changed work, and how it notifies delays. A clear answer here predicts a calmer project. A vague answer predicts arguments.

## How to compare bids like-for-like

Once the bids are in, put them on one page before anyone looks at the bottom line.

| Item | Bidder A | Bidder B | Bidder C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Measured works | | | |
| Preliminaries (and % of total) | | | |
| Provisional sums | | | |
| Exclusions priced back in | | | |
| Qualifications and substitutions | | | |
| Programme duration | | | |
| Payment terms requested | | | |
| Bonds, retention, insurance | | | |
| **Adjusted comparable total** | | | |

Then hold a short clarification meeting with each serious bidder. Ask the same questions of each, record the answers, and have any changes confirmed in writing before you decide. This follows RICS guidance on tendering: queries raised by one bidder are answered to all of them, qualifications and assumptions are reviewed in detail, insurances are checked like-for-like, and a qualification that is resolved is confirmed by the bidder in writing.

Before you compare, also be clear what kind of contract you are pricing. RICS practice information on contract selection describes the main choices for the contract sum as a fixed-price lump sum, remeasurement or a target cost, and the choice changes how much of the bid is really fixed. ## Red flags when choosing a contractor

- A price far below the others, with no clear explanation.
- Pressure to sign quickly, or to pay a deposit before the contract is agreed.
- Reluctance to name the site team or provide references.
- Heavy qualifications, long exclusions or "subject to" language throughout the bid.
- Payment schedules weighted to the front of the job.
- No appetite for bonds, retention or proper insurance.
- Suggestions that work can start before the permit is in place.
- A firm that cannot show you a finished villa of comparable quality.

## What a good contractor wants to see from you

This is the part owners rarely hear. From our years on the builder's side, the best contractors choose their clients as carefully as clients choose them, and they price accordingly.

A good contractor wants to see:

- **Complete drawings and a clear specification.** Uncertainty is priced as risk. The clearer the documents, the sharper the price.
- **A decisive client.** Late selections and changing minds are what delay villas. A contractor who sees an organised decision process prices less contingency.
- **Proof the money is there.** Contractors worry about being paid. Clear payment terms, a sensible cash flow and a track record of paying on time make you a preferred client.
- **A fair contract.** One-sided terms do not make a project safer; they make good contractors walk away and leave the bidders who plan to recover their margin through claims.
- **Someone on the client side who understands construction.** Contractors prefer dealing with a knowledgeable client representative to dealing with confusion. Disputes are expensive for them too.

On the contractor's side, the jobs we priced keenest were the ones where the drawings were finished, the client knew what they wanted, and we trusted we would be paid on time. Those are all things an owner controls.
