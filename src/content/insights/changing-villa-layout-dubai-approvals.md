Changing the layout of a Dubai villa, whether that is removing a wall, opening up a kitchen or dividing a room, almost always needs someone's approval before work starts. In a master community the developer's consent comes first, and work that affects structure, services or the outside of the house is likely to need an authority permit through a registered consultant as well. The safe assumption is that internal work is not exempt.

## Do internal changes really need a permit?

More often than owners expect. Dubai's Local Order No. 3 of 1999, which regulates construction work in the emirate, requires a permit before permanent or temporary works that change land or property, and requires works to follow the approved plans. Writing in Khaleej Times in July 2024, a Dubai lawyer advised that even partitioning a living room to create an extra room falls within this and needs a permit, and that community or master developer rules may require an NOC in addition ([Khaleej Times](https://khaleejtimes.com/uae/legal/dubai-do-property-owners-need-permission-to-add-room-partition)).

This is general information, not legal advice. For your own villa, the answer comes from your developer and the authority for your plot.

## Whose approval do you need?

| Approval | Who gives it | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Developer or community NOC | The master developer or community manager, for example Nakheel or Emaar Community Management | In master communities, before any alteration |
| Building permit | Dubai Municipality, Trakhees or the Dubai Development Authority, depending on your plot | Where the work needs a permit, on an application by a registered consultant |
| Contractor access permits | The community | Before the contractor's team can enter and work |

Nakheel's customer guidance says owners can make changes as long as they do not affect the external façade and structure, and that an NOC from its Urban Planning team is needed before any alteration ([Nakheel](https://www.nakheel.com/en/contact-us)). Emaar Community Management says any alteration to the villa has to be approved by Emaar, and that contractors need community entry permits even for work that needs no NOC ([Emaar Community Management](https://www.ecm.ae/en/communities/arabian-ranches/lifestyle/faqs/home-modifications-arabian-ranches/)).

Which authority issues your permit depends on the plot, not the community name. Our guide to [which Dubai authority covers your villa](/insights/which-dubai-authority-covers-my-villa/) explains how to find out. Permit applications for all three run through Dubai's unified building permit window ([Dubai Municipality](https://www.dm.gov.ae/dubai-launches-single-window-for-building-permits/)).

## Which layout changes carry the most risk?

The table below is a guide to how much scrutiny to expect, not a statement of the rules for your community. Confirm each item in writing before work starts.

| Change | What it touches | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Removing or opening a load-bearing wall | Structure | Structural design by a registered engineer, developer consent and an authority permit |
| Removing a non-structural partition | Layout, sometimes services in the wall | Developer consent; confirm in writing whether a permit is needed |
| Adding a partition or a room inside the house | Layout, ventilation, escape routes | Approval is required; see the Khaleej Times guidance above |
| Moving a kitchen or bathroom | Drainage, water, ventilation, sometimes gas | Services design and approval; drainage falls limit where wet rooms can go |
| Enclosing a balcony, terrace or garage | Built-up area and the outside of the house | Treated as an extension in most communities; see our [villa extension guide](/insights/villa-extension-dubai/) |
| Changing windows, doors or openings | The façade | Developer design control as well as any permit |
| New finishes, joinery and decoration only | Nothing structural | Usually the lightest route, but access permits still apply |

## Why you should never take a contractor's word on a wall

On the contractor's side we saw this more than once: a wall described as "just a partition" that turned out to carry a beam, or to hide a drainage stack, a riser or the main air-conditioning ducts. Villas built in the same community are not always built the same way, and earlier owners may have altered them.

Before anything is removed:

- **Get the approved drawings.** Ask the developer or the authority for the approved plans, and compare them with the house as it stands.
- **Ask a registered structural engineer.** Whether a wall carries load is their decision, given in writing, not the contractor's.
- **Open up before you commit.** A small inspection opening tells you what is inside the wall before the design depends on it.
- **Check the services.** Moving a wall often means moving cables, pipes, ducts and sprinklers or detectors.

## What happens if work is done without approval?

Unapproved alterations do not disappear. They tend to surface when you apply for your next NOC or permit, when the community inspects, or when you sell and the buyer's surveyor compares the house with the approved plans. Putting it right afterwards usually costs more than doing it properly, and can mean opening up finished work.

If you are buying an older villa, ask the seller for the approvals and as-built drawings for any changes. Our guide to [renovating an older villa's services](/insights/older-villa-mep-renovation/) covers what else to check.

## What is the right order?

1. **Brief and survey.** Decide what you want, and survey the house as built.
2. **Design.** Have the layout drawn, with structural and services input where needed.
3. **Developer consent.** Apply for the NOC with the drawings.
4. **Authority permit.** Where required, your registered consultant applies.
5. **Access permits and deposits.** The contractor arranges these with the community.
6. **Work, inspections and close-out.** Keep the approvals, completion sign-offs and as-built drawings together.
