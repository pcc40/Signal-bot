Design and build can work well for a Dubai villa, and the argument for it is partly right: one contract does close the gap between designer and builder, and that gap causes many disputes. What it does not do is make the price free of risk, or give you anyone independent checking the design, the price or the payments. If you choose it, the protection comes from what you write into the contract and from who reviews the contractor's work for you.

This article sets out the two routes fairly, the risks that move between them, and what to put in place if you go with a design-and-build firm. It is general information, not legal advice; take advice from a UAE-qualified lawyer before you sign.

## What is the difference between design and build and a separate architect?

On the **traditional, consultant-led route**, you appoint a registered engineering consultancy to design the villa. The design is priced by contractors, and you sign a construction contract with the one you choose. The consultant works for you and normally carries the statutory site role during construction. FIDIC's Red Book is written for this arrangement: "building and engineering works where the employer bears the design responsibility" ([FIDIC](https://fidic.org/sites/default/files/press%20release_rainbow%20suite_2018_03.pdf)).

On the **design-and-build route**, one firm takes responsibility for both the design and the construction under a single contract. You describe what you want in a set of employer's requirements, the contractor answers with its proposals and a price, and it then develops the design and builds it. FIDIC's Yellow Book is written for this: works "designed by the contractor" ([FIDIC](https://fidic.org/books/plant-and-design-build-contract-2nd-ed-2017-yellow-book-reprinted-2022-amendments)). Its Silver Book goes further and puts almost all design and construction risk on the contractor for a fixed price.

In Dubai, one thing does not change between the routes. Under Law No. 14 of 2025, engineering consultancy across the emirate, including free zones, may only be practised by registered offices ([Dubai Municipality](https://www.dm.gov.ae/documents/law-no-14-of-2025-regulating-engineering-consultancy-practice-in-the-emirate-of-dubai-municipality/); [Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/news/dubai-issues-new-law-regulating-engineering-consultancy-activities-key-takeaway-points/)). A design-and-build firm still needs a registered consultancy to design the villa. The question is who appoints it.

## Where does design and build genuinely help?

It is worth being clear about what integrated firms do well, because the case for them is not empty.

- **One contract and one point of responsibility.** If the design does not work on site, there is no argument between architect and builder about whose fault it is. The contractor owns both.
- **Buildability.** The people pricing and building the villa shape the design, so details tend to suit the way the contractor works.
- **Speed.** Design and procurement can overlap, and some packages can start before every drawing is finished.
- **Fewer interfaces for the owner.** You manage one relationship instead of two or three.

On a simple, well-defined villa, with an owner who knows what they want and has the time to follow the job, those advantages can outweigh the drawbacks.

## What risk does design and build transfer, and what does it cost?

The single point of accountability is real. But risk is never moved for nothing. A contractor that takes on design and coordination risk prices it. A paper published by FIDIC on its Silver Book puts it plainly: a fixed price of this kind "will include contingency allowances to hedge against the risk of things costing more or taking longer", and owners "expect to pay a premium" for it ([Hosie, FIDIC](https://fidic.org/sites/default/files/hosie07.pdf)). The Silver Book also deems the contractor to have checked the employer's requirements and makes it responsible for their accuracy, with only limited exceptions. That is a large risk, and it is priced accordingly.

So "costs no more" can be true, and it can be false. It depends on three things:

1. **How complete your requirements are.** Thin requirements leave the contractor free to choose the cheapest compliant answer, and anything better becomes a change.
2. **How much competition there is.** When each bidder prices its own design, the quotes are hard to compare like-for-like, and some owners end up negotiating with one firm.
3. **How changes are defined.** RICS guidance on design and build notes that a contractor developing its own design to build the works or meet statutory requirements is not a change ([RICS](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/employers_agent_design_and_build_1st_edition.pdf)). Equally, anything you ask for beyond the employer's requirements is. Where that line sits decides who pays.

On the contractor's side, we priced design-and-build work in the UK for many years. Our founder, Philip Chapman, ran P Chapman Construction for 21 years. The first thing we read was the employer's requirements, and the thinner they were, the more room there was to make margin through design choices the client would never see. That is not dishonest. It is how a fixed price with design risk works, and owners should go in knowing it.

## Who checks the builder's design?

This is the honest weakness of the route. Under design and build, the designer works for the contractor. RICS guidance says the employer's representative under a design-and-build contract is "not responsible for and not required to approve drawings or design development", and must not interfere with the contractor's control of its design team ([RICS](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/employers_agent_design_and_build_1st_edition.pdf)). The same guidance notes that some employers keep a "shadow" design team, such as an architect or engineer, to support them through the project.

In practice you have three ways to keep the design honest:

- **Keep your own registered consultant** to review the contractor's design against your requirements, the Dubai Building Code and your budget. This costs a fee, but it gives you a designer who works for you.
- **Write review periods into the contract**, so that design packages come to you for comment before work proceeds. Make it "review and comment" rather than approval, so the contractor's design responsibility stays where it belongs.
- **Have an owner's representative review the commercial side**: specification changes, value engineering proposals, allowances and the effect of each design decision on cost and programme.

Over-design is a risk on any route, not only this one. In August 2025 Dubai Municipality warned consultancies that some villa structural designs went beyond the Dubai Building Code's safety requirements without a proven need, raising construction costs ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/uae/dubai-municipality-warns-consultancies-inflating-villa-designs-raising-costs-1.500245704)). Under design and build the incentive usually runs the other way, towards the leanest compliant design. Either way, someone on your side should be asking why.

## Who checks what under each route?

The table below shows the typical arrangement on a private villa. Contracts vary, and in Dubai a registered consultancy designs the villa and holds the statutory role on every route.

| | Contractor-led | Design and build | Consultant-led | Client-side PM alongside any route |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Who checks the quote** | Usually the owner alone, comparing totals | The owner, unless an adviser is appointed; bids on different designs are hard to compare | The consultant, against its own drawings and specification | Reviews the quote line by line and compares bids like-for-like; the owner decides |
| **Variations** | Priced by the contractor and agreed directly with the owner | Priced by the contractor; "design development" claims are argued by the owner | Assessed by the consultant under the contract | Reviews each variation against the contract, keeps the register and recommends to the owner |
| **Payments** | Owner pays against the contractor's applications | Owner pays against the contractor's own valuations | Consultant checks applications against progress | Reviews applications against progress seen on site and recommends; the owner pays direct |
| **Quality** | The contractor's own checks and authority inspections | The contractor's team and the consultancy it appoints | The owner's consultant, in its statutory role | Observes and records on site, and raises concerns for the registered consultant to confirm |
| **Who you call** | The contractor's owner or site manager | The firm's project manager, who reports to the firm | The consultant's project architect or engineer | The client-side PM, who reports only to you |

For a fuller explanation of the roles, see [owner's representative, project manager or consultant](/insights/owners-representative-vs-project-manager-vs-consultant/).

## What should you put in a design-and-build contract?

If you choose design and build, these are the points we would want settled before signing. Dubai Municipality's [guidance contract forms for private villas](https://www.dm.gov.ae/municipality-business/guidance-contracts-forms-for-engineering-consulting-services-and-construction-contracting-for-private-villas/), in Arabic, are a sensible baseline, and a UAE-qualified lawyer should review any amendments.

- **Detailed employer's requirements.** Room schedule, finishes, systems, performance standards and the budget for allowances. If you want the villa to be fit for a stated purpose, say so in writing. Al Tamimi notes that under UAE law there is no implied term that a contract must be fit for purpose, and that a contractor may meet its obligation by exercising reasonable care unless the contract says otherwise ([Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update/may-10/articles/fit-for-purpose-or-reasonable-care-dealing-with-design-liability-in-the-new-fidic-yellow-book/)). The same article notes that most professional indemnity policies only cover negligence, which limits what a strict obligation is worth in practice.
- **Order of precedence.** Which document wins if the employer's requirements and the contractor's proposals disagree.
- **Design submissions and review periods.** What comes to you, when, and how long you have to comment.
- **The registered consultancy.** Who appoints it, who pays it, and whether it will owe you a duty directly.
- **A clear definition of a change**, separating design development, which is the contractor's cost, from changes you instruct, which are yours.
- **Listed [provisional sums](/glossary/#provisional-sum) and allowances**, each with a figure and a rule for how it is adjusted.
- **A payment schedule tied to work done**, not to design milestones that let money run ahead of progress, with [retention](/glossary/#retention) and bonds defined.
- **A licence to use the drawings** if the contract ends early, so you can finish the villa with someone else.
- **Handover documents**: as-built drawings, warranties, manuals and authority completion paperwork.

Design and build does not change the law on structural safety. UAE civil law makes the contractor and the architect or engineer who oversaw the works jointly liable for ten years for collapse or defects that threaten the building's stability, and that liability cannot be excluded by contract ([Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update/december-january-2/articles/decennial-liability-in-the-uae-a-commentary/)). The new Civil Transactions Law that took effect on 1 June 2026 keeps this regime, according to commentary ([Legal 500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-arab-emirates/real-estate-and-construction/uae-construction-contracts-key-changes-and-dispute-risks-under-the-2026-civil-transactions-law)). See [decennial liability](/glossary/#decennial-liability) in our glossary, and our guide to [villa construction contracts in the UAE](/insights/villa-construction-contracts-uae/) for variations, delays and payment.

## Using a design-and-build contractor? What an owner's representative still does

An in-house project manager at a design-and-build firm may be very good. But they report to the firm's owner, and their job is to deliver the contract the firm signed. That is not a criticism; it is the structure. Nobody in it is paid to ask whether you are getting value.

Alongside a design-and-build contractor, a client-side project manager:

- **reviews the employer's requirements** before tender, so the gaps are closed while they are cheap to close
- **runs or benchmarks the tender**, so you can compare bids that rest on different designs
- **reviews payment applications** against progress seen on site, and recommends what to pay
- **reviews variations and design development claims** against the contract, and keeps the register
- **owns the decision log**, so every choice you make is recorded with its cost and programme effect
- **observes and records on site**, and raises quality concerns for the registered consultant to confirm
- **advises when things go wrong**, from delay notices to a stalled job

It does not redesign the villa, manage the contractor's team or instruct the contractor; you remain the one who instructs. If the villa is simple and you can follow it closely yourself, you may not need this at all. On a bespoke villa, or when you live abroad, it is usually where the value is.

## Which route is right for your villa?

Design and build tends to suit a villa with a clear, well-defined brief, a conventional specification and an owner who wants speed and one contract. A separate architect tends to suit a bespoke villa, a design you want to own and shape, or a site with unusual constraints, where you want a designer who works only for you. Either can work. What decides the outcome is whether someone independent is checking the price, the payments and the changes.
