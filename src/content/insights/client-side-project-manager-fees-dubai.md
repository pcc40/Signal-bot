A client-side project manager in Dubai will usually charge in one of three ways: a fixed fee for a defined review, a monthly fee, or a percentage of construction cost. The headline number matters less than three details behind it: what the percentage is applied to, who actually does the work and how often they are on site, and what happens to the fee if the build overruns. There is no published Dubai benchmark for these fees, so the only fair comparison is proposal against proposal, each priced against the same written scope of works.

This guide explains the fee models, the small print that changes what you pay, and the questions to ask any firm, including us. Our own published ranges are on our [fees page](/fees/).

## How do client-side project managers charge?

The Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors (RICS) guidance note *Appointing a project manager* (GN 107/2013) groups project management appointments into four generic types: lump sum, time based, target, and term or framework ([RICS GN 107/2013](https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/appointing_a_project_manager_1st_edition_rics.pdf)). It notes that a lump sum can be expressed as a single figure, a schedule of prices, or a percentage. On a private villa, those reduce to three models in practice.

| Fee model | How it works | Suits | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed fee for a review | One price for a defined piece of work, such as a plot, design, contractor-quote or stalled-project review, ending in a written report | A single decision point | What the report covers, and whether a follow-up meeting is included |
| Monthly fee | A set amount each month for an agreed level of input | Projects where length or scope is uncertain | What happens when the programme runs long |
| Percentage of construction cost | A percentage of the build cost, paid in stages | A well-defined build from start to handover | What the percentage is applied to |

RICS also describes time-based (reimbursable) appointments, where you pay hours at agreed rates, and target arrangements that share savings or excesses. These are more common on larger commercial work. For time-based appointments, RICS suggests an "upset limit": a ceiling the charges cannot pass without your prior written agreement. The same idea works on any model.

FIDIC's Client/Consultant Model Services Agreement, the White Book (5th edition, 2017), is the international standard form most often used to appoint consultants. FIDIC describes it as "one of the most widely used forms of construction professional services contract currently being used globally", suitable for services including project management and the administration of construction ([FIDIC](https://fidic.org/node/42966)). It does not set fee levels. Its value to an owner is the structure: a defined scope of services, a procedure for varying that scope and agreeing the effect on the consultant's remuneration, and a programme the consultant must follow ([Curtis](https://omanlawblog.curtis.com/2017/10/whats-new-in-2017-fidic-white-book.html)).

## What is a percentage fee calculated on?

This is the question most owners never ask, and it can move the fee more than the percentage itself. RICS is direct: when using a percentage, "it is important to define and agree what the percentage will be applied to, e.g. the approved budget, the accepted tender for the construction contract or the final cost of the works."

Each choice behaves differently:

- **The approved budget.** The fee is known from the start. If the build later comes in under budget, you still pay on the budget.
- **The accepted tender.** The fee is fixed once the contract is signed. It does not rise when variations are added, so the project manager has no financial reason to welcome them.
- **The final cost of the works.** The fee follows the actual cost. That sounds fair, but it means the fee rises when the cost rises.

Ask also what "construction cost" includes. Does it cover loose furniture, landscaping, the pool, specialist joinery bought directly by you, or authority and connection fees? Each inclusion widens the base the percentage is applied to.

RICS adds a caution worth repeating: "As the complexity of the project increases the use of percentage of construction cost becomes less reliable." On a truly one-off villa, where design and specification keep developing, a percentage is a reasonable guide but a weak contract on its own. It needs a written scope beside it.

There is a wider point here. A fee that grows with the build cost sits awkwardly with the job of controlling that cost. In August 2025, Dubai Municipality warned several engineering consultancy offices that villa structural designs had gone "beyond safety requirements in the Dubai Building Code" and "ended up unjustifiably raising construction costs" for homeowners ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/uae/dubai-municipality-warns-consultancies-inflating-villa-designs-raising-costs-1.500245704)). That warning was about designers, not project managers, but the lesson carries: understand how everyone you appoint is paid, and whether a higher build cost pays them more.

## Who will do the work, and how often will they be on site?

A project management fee is mostly the cost of people's time. Two proposals at the same percentage can buy very different things if one names a senior person attending site regularly and the other names nobody.

RICS lists "key (and other) personnel" among the elements every appointment should include. In its typical criteria for assessing project management proposals, "key personnel (qualifications and experience relative to proposed role)" carries the largest suggested weighting, 30% to 40% of the quality score. The people are the service.

Ask any firm to put in writing:

- The person who leads your project, by name, and what they personally do.
- Who covers when that person is unavailable.
- How often someone attends site at each stage, and which meetings they attend.
- What you receive after each visit, and each month.
- Whether the named lead can be replaced without your agreement.

At Eastbridge, our founder, Philip Chapman, leads every appointment personally, supported by a team of three project managers who attend site and provide cover. Site attendance is agreed in writing in the scope of each appointment.

From the builder's side, we can tell you how this looked on the other side of the table. On a contractor's team, you quickly learn which client representatives actually turn up, and which ones read the monthly report and nothing else. The ones who appeared at the right moments, before concrete was poured and before walls were closed up, were the ones whose questions we prepared for. Site presence changes behaviour in a way that a report alone does not.

## What is usually excluded from a project manager's fee?

Exclusions are where like-for-like comparison fails most often. Read them as carefully as the price. Common exclusions on a Dubai villa include:

- **The registered consultant's fee.** Design and the statutory engineering role belong to a Dubai Municipality-registered consultancy. Under Dubai Law No. 14 of 2025, only registered offices may practise engineering consultancy or present themselves as consultancy offices ([Dubai Media Office](https://www.mediaoffice.ae/en/news/2025/october/05-10/mohammed-bin-rashid), [Law No. 14 of 2025](https://www.dm.gov.ae/documents/law-no-14-of-2025-regulating-engineering-consultancy-practice-in-the-emirate-of-dubai-municipality/)). That appointment is separate, with its own fee. Dubai Municipality publishes guidance contract forms for consultancy and contracting on private villas, in Arabic, which it describes as for guidance rather than mandatory ([Dubai Municipality](https://www.dm.gov.ae/municipality-business/guidance-contracts-forms-for-engineering-consulting-services-and-construction-contracting-for-private-villas/)).
- **Other specialists** such as a quantity surveyor, a structural or condition survey, or a UAE-qualified lawyer, appointed and paid by the owner.
- **Authority, master developer and utility fees**, which are paid by the owner whoever prepares the paperwork.
- **Travel**, especially overseas trips to source stone, joinery, lighting or fittings. Check whether it is charged at cost or with a margin.
- **Disputes.** Work on a claim, adjudication or arbitration is often excluded or charged separately.
- **The defects period.** Some appointments end at handover; others run to the end of the defects liability period.
- **VAT.** UAE VAT is charged at the standard rate of 5% on taxable supplies of services, unless an exemption or zero rate applies ([Federal Tax Authority, Decree-Law No. 8 of 2017, Article 3](https://tax.gov.ae/DataFolder/Files/Pdf/VAT-Decree-Law-No-8-of-2017.pdf)). Ask whether a quote includes it.

## What happens to the fee if the build overruns?

Agree this before anything goes wrong. Overruns come in two forms, cost and time, and each fee model reacts differently.

**Cost overruns.** A percentage applied to the final cost rises with it. As an illustration only, using our published full-service range: on a build of AED 12 million, a fee of 2.5% is AED 300,000. If the final cost rises by 10% to AED 13.2 million, the same fee applied to the final cost becomes AED 330,000. Applied to the accepted tender, it stays at AED 300,000. Neither is wrong, but you should know which you have signed.

**Time overruns.** A monthly fee rises with every extra month: a four-month delay adds four months of fees. Percentage fees can also extend in practice, because a firm that has priced eighteen months of attendance will reasonably ask to be paid for twenty-four.

The FIDIC White Book shows how a standard form handles this. Clause 4.4 of the 2017 edition gives the consultant additional fees for "delay, impediment or prevention caused or attributable to the Client" and to "the Client's other consultants, contractors, or other third parties" ([Beale & Co, via ACE](https://www.acenet.co.uk/news/affiliate/prolongation-and-fair-recourse-for-consultants/)). In plain terms, a contractor's delay can increase your project manager's fee. That is fair, because the extra months are real work, but it should be visible and priced in advance.

Points to agree in writing:

- Which base the percentage is applied to, and whether it is fixed at the accepted tender.
- A ceiling, or "upset limit", that the fee cannot pass without your written agreement.
- How extra months are priced if the programme extends, and who decides that it has extended.
- Whether part of the fee is tied to stages, so you are not paying ahead of progress.
- On long projects, how rates are reviewed. RICS recommends that rates and prices be fixed for no more than four years, with an annual review against an appropriate index preferable.

## What questions should you ask any project manager about fees?

Ask every firm the same questions, including us, and ask for the answers in writing. Fees are only part of the choice: our guide to [choosing a client-side project manager](/insights/how-to-choose-client-side-project-manager/) covers independence, who does the work and how to compare firms.

1. What exactly is in the scope of works, and what is outside it?
2. Is the fee fixed, monthly or a percentage? If a percentage, what is it applied to, and what does "construction cost" include?
3. Who leads my project, by name, and who else will work on it?
4. How often will someone attend site at each stage, and what will I receive afterwards?
5. What happens to the fee if the cost or the programme overruns?
6. How is work outside the scope priced, and will I see that price before the work starts?
7. What is excluded: travel, other specialists, disputes, the defects period, VAT?
8. Do you receive any payment from contractors, consultants or suppliers on my project?
9. Can you instruct the contractor or approve a payment without my written approval?
10. How does the appointment end, and what do I receive at the end?

The last two matter more than they look. RICS lists "authority levels" among the key elements of an appointment. The person representing you should not be able to spend your money or commit you to a change without your written approval.
